## New lesson-note lines

Conventional current points in the direction a positive charge would move: from the positive terminal towards the negative terminal through the external circuit. In a metal, the moving carriers are negative electrons, which drift towards the positive terminal, opposite to conventional current.
*Source: SaveMyExams/9702 as-physics-cie-25/9702 c9.1.pdf, pp.2, 6–7.*
> [!example]- Worked example: total electron count
> A uniform wire of length $20\,\mathrm{cm}$ contains $N=3.0\times10^{22}$ free electrons. Their average drift speed is $v=2.0\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Calculate the current magnitude without knowing the cross-sectional area.
> Convert the length: $L=20\,\mathrm{cm}=0.20\,\mathrm m$. The wire volume is $AL$, so $n=N/(AL)$.
> Substitute into $I=Anvq$, using the electron charge magnitude $q=e$:
> $$
> I=A\left(\frac{N}{AL}\right)ve=\frac{Nev}{L}.
> $$
> The area cancels; divide by length because $N$ is the count in the whole wire, not the count per unit length.
> $$
> I=\frac{(3.0\times10^{22})(1.60\times10^{-19})(2.0\times10^{-5})}{0.20}=0.48\,\mathrm A.
> $$
> Check: for fixed $N$, $A$ and $v$, a larger $L$ means a larger volume and a smaller number density, so the current decreases. Doubling $L$ halves $n$ and $I$.
*Method sources: SaveMyExams/9702 as-physics-cie-25/9702 c9.1.pdf, pp.7–8; ER 9702 s21 P13 Q33. Official equation and elementary charge checked against the [Cambridge 2025–2027 syllabus](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org/Images/664565-2025-2027-syllabus.pdf), pp.23, 58.*
