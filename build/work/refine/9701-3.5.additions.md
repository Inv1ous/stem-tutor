## New lesson-note lines

Ozone, $\ce{O3}$, has **two bonding regions plus one lone pair** on its central oxygen: **three regions**, even though a Lewis structure shows a double bond. Its molecule is bent and its angle is slightly below $120°$, about $117°$ in the cited exam question (ER 9701 w22 P12 Q13). Ammonia has **three bonding regions plus one lone pair**: **four regions**, giving $107°$. Water has **two bonding regions plus two lone pairs**: **four regions**, giving $104.5°$. Neither sharing a lone-pair count with ammonia nor sharing a bent shape with water makes ozone's angle the same.
> [!example]- Worked electron count: $\ce{NH2-}$
> Start with nitrogen's five valence electrons. Add one for each H and one for the negative charge: $5+2+1=8$ electrons in total. A positive charge would instead remove an electron per unit charge.
> Assign two electrons to each N–H bond: the two bonds use four electrons. The remaining $8-4=4$ electrons form two lone pairs on nitrogen.
> There are two bonding regions and two lone pairs: four regions altogether, arranged roughly tetrahedrally. Name the shape from atom positions only: **bent (non-linear)**, with a predicted angle about $105°$ by analogy with water.
> For structures with multiple bonds, allocate all their bonding electrons when drawing the Lewis structure, but count each multiple bond as **one region** when predicting shape.
