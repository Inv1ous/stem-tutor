## New lesson-note lines

### Why the trends occur
1. Across Period 3, nuclear charge increases while shielding remains similar because added electrons enter the same main shell. Attraction for bonding electrons increases, so electronegativity rises.
2. The electronegativity difference from oxygen or chlorine generally decreases: oxide and chloride bonding becomes more covalent across the period.
3. Ionic metal oxides contain basic $\ce{O^2-}$ ions that accept protons. Sodium oxide reacts readily with water; magnesium oxide reacts only slightly and leaves a suspension. $\ce{Al2O3}$ is amphoteric: it reacts with acids and aqueous NaOH despite being insoluble in water.
4. Molecular phosphorus and sulfur oxides react with water to form acids and neutralise bases. Giant covalent $\ce{SiO2}$ is an important exception to any claim that all covalent oxides react with water. For chlorides, NaCl dissolves without hydrolysis, hydrated aluminium ions acidify water, and molecular $\ce{SiCl4}$ and $\ce{PCl5}$ hydrolyse. Bonding predicts broad trends, but structure and reaction conditions still matter.
> [!example]- Worked contrast: dissolution or hydrolysis?
> **NaCl:** $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$. Water hydrates existing ions; no hydrolysis produces NaOH or HCl. There is one Na and one Cl on each side, and the total charge is zero. The solution is approximately pH 7.
> **Silicon chloride:** $\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}$. Hydrolysis gives a white silica solid in an acidic aqueous phase; steamy fumes may appear during addition. Each side contains one Si, four Cl, four H and two O atoms.
> The white solid is not dissolved silica. Dissolved HCl forms $\ce{H3O+(aq)}$, which causes acidity; chloride ions alone do not make NaCl solution acidic. Use (s) for silica and (aq) for HCl retained in excess water.
