## New lesson-note lines

### Work backwards to check a volume
For a roughly cubic or spherical object, $V^{1/3}$ gives a characteristic length: a volume in $\mathrm{cm^3}$ gives a length in $\mathrm{cm}$. Compare this with the object's familiar size. For a cube it is the side; for a sphere it is only a size check, with exact diameter $d=(6V/\pi)^{1/3}$.
For example, proposed football volumes of a few hundred, a few thousand and a few tens of thousands of cubic centimetres imply very different sizes. Using representative values $600$, $6000$ and $60000\ \mathrm{cm^3}$ gives cube-root lengths about $8$, $18$ and $39\ \mathrm{cm}$. The middle scale fits a football about $22\ \mathrm{cm}$ across; the others are too small or too large. This reverses the usual length-to-volume estimate.
*Method source: Cambridge examiner report, 9702 Nov 2020 P13 Q1, as quoted in the chapter bundle; explanation paraphrased.*
### Reusable scale anchors
These are approximate checks, not exact data to substitute when a question supplies values.
| Quantity | Approximate anchor |
|---|---|
| Mass of a small car | $10^3\ \mathrm{kg}$ |
| Speed of sound in air | $3\times10^2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ |
| Atmospheric pressure near sea level | $10^5\ \mathrm{Pa}$ |
*Library source: sources/library/SaveMyExams/9702 a-level-physics-cie-25/9702 c1.1.pdf, p.3. Table-practice source: sources/library/SaveMyExams/TQ 9702 a-level-physics-cie-25-questions-only/TQ 9702 c1.1.pdf, pp.18–19; new practice questions use original wording.*
The air anchors depend on conditions: pressure decreases with altitude, and sound speed depends on temperature. NASA gives a standard sea-level pressure about $101\ \mathrm{kPa}$ and sound speed near room temperature about $340\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, supporting the rounded checks above ([NASA air properties](https://www1.grc.nasa.gov/beginners-guide-to-aeronautics/air-properties-definitions/), [NASA sound explanation](https://cosmicopia.gsfc.nasa.gov/qa_gp_ls.html)).
