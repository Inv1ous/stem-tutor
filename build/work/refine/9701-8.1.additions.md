## New lesson-note lines

![[Assets/9701/9701-8.1-rate-gradients.svg]]
The chord joins the curve at $10$ and $60\ \mathrm{s}$: its gradient gives the average formation rate over that interval. The tangent touches the curve at $20\ \mathrm{s}$: its gradient gives the instantaneous formation rate there. The curve becomes less steep as the reaction slows.
> [!example]- Worked example: instantaneous disappearance rate
> A tangent at $50.0\ \mathrm{s}$ on a reactant concentration-time curve passes through $(10.0\ \mathrm{s},\ 0.360\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}})$ and $(90.0\ \mathrm{s},\ 0.120\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}})$. Calculate the instantaneous disappearance rate at $50.0\ \mathrm{s}$.
> Use these well-separated points on the tangent; they need not lie on the curve itself.
> Gradient $=\Delta c/\Delta t=(0.120-0.360)/(90.0-10.0)=-0.240/80.0=-0.00300\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$.
> The negative gradient means the reactant concentration is falling. Its positive disappearance rate is $-\Delta c/\Delta t=3.00\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$ at the instant where the tangent was drawn, not an average over the curve from $10$ to $90\ \mathrm{s}$.
