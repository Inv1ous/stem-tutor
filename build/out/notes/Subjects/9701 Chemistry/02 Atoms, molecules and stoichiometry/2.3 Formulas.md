# 2.3 Formulas
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A chemical formula records either the ratio of ions in a neutral compound or the atoms in a molecule. Chemical equations conserve atoms and charge; calculations turn measured masses into mole ratios before choosing subscripts.

## Key ideas
An **ionic formula** has zero overall charge. Predict common ion charges from periodic-table groups: Group 1 forms $1+$, Group 2 forms $2+$, Group 13 metals such as aluminium form $3+$, Group 16 nonmetals commonly form $2-$, and Group 17 nonmetals form $1-$. A Roman numeral states a metal's oxidation number: iron(III) is $\ce{Fe^3+}$. Balance charge using the smallest whole-number ion ratio. For example, $\ce{Fe^3+}$ and $\ce{SO4^2-}$ form $\ce{Fe2(SO4)3}$; parentheses show that sulfate stays intact.

Learn these ions by name and formula: nitrate $\ce{NO3-}$, carbonate $\ce{CO3^2-}$, sulfate $\ce{SO4^2-}$, hydroxide $\ce{OH-}$, ammonium $\ce{NH4+}$, zinc $\ce{Zn^2+}$, silver $\ce{Ag+}$, hydrogencarbonate $\ce{HCO3-}$ and phosphate $\ce{PO4^3-}$. The charges determine the formula; a subscript never represents a charge by itself.

The **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound. The **molecular formula** gives the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule. For glucose these are $\ce{CH2O}$ and $\ce{C6H12O6}$ respectively. $\ce{P2O5}$ is the empirical formula of phosphorus(V) oxide; its molecular formula is $\ce{P4O10}$.

A **hydrated** crystalline salt contains **water of crystallisation**: water incorporated in a fixed ratio within its crystal structure. **Anhydrous** means no water of crystallisation. In $\ce{CuSO4.5H2O}$, five waters belong to each formula unit. Heating may remove them, provided the salt does not decompose.

## Method
1. To write an ionic formula, write both ions and charges, find the least common magnitude of charge, choose the smallest numbers of ions that cancel, and use brackets if more than one polyatomic ion is needed.
2. To balance an equation, write correct formulas first, then adjust coefficients only. Check every element. Add state symbols: $(s)$ solid, $(l)$ liquid, $(g)$ gas and $(aq)$ aqueous. In a net ionic equation, split soluble strong electrolytes into ions and cancel spectator ions unchanged on both sides. For example, $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}$.
3. To find an empirical formula, divide each elemental mass or percentage by its $A_r$. Divide all resulting mole amounts by the smallest. If necessary multiply to obtain the simplest whole-number ratio. To find a molecular formula, divide the given $M_r$ by empirical formula mass and multiply every empirical subscript by this integer.
4. For a hydrate $\ce{salt.xH2O}$, divide water lost by $M_r(\ce{H2O})$ and dry-salt mass by its formula mass. The ratio $n(\ce{H2O})/n(\ce{salt})$ gives $x$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A compound contains 40.0% C, 6.7% H and 53.3% O. Its $M_r$ is 180. Find both formulas.
> $$n(\ce{C}):n(\ce{H}):n(\ce{O})=40.0/12:6.7/1:53.3/16\approx 1:2:1$$
> The empirical formula is $\ce{CH2O}$. Its formula mass is $12+2+16=30$.
> $$180/30=6,\qquad \text{molecular formula}=\ce{C6H12O6}$$

## Traps
- **Trap:** Use the mass percentages as atom numbers. **Why it's wrong:** Different atoms have different masses. **Instead:** Divide each mass by $A_r$ first.
- **Trap:** Change a formula subscript while balancing an equation. **Why it's wrong:** That changes the substance. **Instead:** Change coefficients only.
- **Trap:** Include spectator ions in a net ionic equation. **Why it's wrong:** They undergo no change. **Instead:** Cancel identical ions on both sides.
- **Trap:** Treat the empirical formula as molecular. **Why it's wrong:** The molecular formula may be a multiple. **Instead:** Use the measured $M_r$.

## Exam technique
For **Deduce**, show the charge or mole ratio that fixes the formula. For **Calculate**, show division by $A_r$ or formula mass before rounding to small whole numbers. In equation questions, check both atom counts and charge, include state symbols where appropriate, and keep polyatomic ions intact while writing formulas. If a hydration question gives crucible masses, subtract the empty crucible mass to find the dry salt and subtract the final mass from the initial mass to find water lost.
