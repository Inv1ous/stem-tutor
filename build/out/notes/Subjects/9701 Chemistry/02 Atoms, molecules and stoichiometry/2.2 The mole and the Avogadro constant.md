# 2.2 The mole and the Avogadro constant
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A mole is a way to count an enormous number of specified particles. One mole always contains the same number of entities, but different substances have different masses per mole.

## Key ideas
**One mole** is the amount of substance containing $6.02\times10^{23}$ specified elementary entities. This number per mole is the **Avogadro constant**, $N_A=6.02\times10^{23}\ \mathrm{mol^{-1}}$. The entities may be atoms, molecules, ions, electrons or formula units. State which ones you mean: one mole of water molecules and one mole of water atoms are different amounts of water.

For an amount $n$ in mol and a number $N$ of specified entities,
$$N=nN_A\qquad n=\frac{N}{N_A}.$$
Particle number $N$ is a count with no unit. The unit $\mathrm{mol^{-1}}$ on $N_A$ ensures that multiplying by mol produces a count.

A chemical formula gives the number of atoms in each molecule or formula unit. One mole of $\ce{H2O}$ molecules contains two moles of hydrogen atoms and one mole of oxygen atoms. One mole of $\ce{CO2}$ molecules contains three moles of atoms in total. One mole of $\ce{NaCl}$ formula units contains one mole of sodium ions and one mole of chloride ions, or two moles of ions altogether. Always do this second count when the question names atoms or ions rather than molecules or formula units.

## Method
1. Underline the named entity in the question. If a mass is given, first obtain the amount of substance with $n=m/M$, where $M$ is the molar mass found using the $A_r$ values on the paper.
2. Convert the amount of molecules or formula units to a particle count with $N=nN_A$.
3. If the question asks for a constituent atom or ion, multiply by its number in each molecule or formula unit. If given a particle count, reverse these steps and divide by $N_A$.
4. Give a sensible number of significant figures and check the kind of entity in your answer.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Calculate the number of oxygen atoms in $0.250\ \mathrm{mol}$ of $\ce{CO2}$ molecules.
> Each molecule contains two oxygen atoms, so the amount of oxygen atoms is $2\times0.250=0.500\ \mathrm{mol}$.
> $$N=0.500\times(6.02\times10^{23})=3.01\times10^{23}\text{ oxygen atoms}.$$
> The answer counts atoms. The number of $\ce{CO2}$ molecules would be half as large.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating a mole as a fixed mass. **Why it's wrong:** The mole fixes particle number; mass depends on molar mass. **Instead:** Name the entities and use $M$ only when converting mass to amount.
- **Trap:** Using the molecule count as the atom count. **Why it's wrong:** Molecules can have several atoms. **Instead:** Multiply by the relevant subscript or the sum of all subscripts.
- **Trap:** Counting one ion per formula unit of an ionic compound. **Why it's wrong:** A formula unit can contain several ions. **Instead:** Read the formula and count each ion separately.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, state that one mole contains $6.02\times10^{23}$ specified entities. For **Calculate**, show the amount in mol, the conversion by $N_A$, and any formula multiplier. A question asking for oxygen **atoms** needs that multiplier even when its starting quantity is in moles of molecules. When comparing samples, convert each to moles of the *named* entity before deciding which count is larger.
