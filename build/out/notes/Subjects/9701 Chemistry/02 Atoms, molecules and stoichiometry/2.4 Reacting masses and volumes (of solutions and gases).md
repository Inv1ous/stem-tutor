# 2.4 Reacting masses and volumes (of solutions and gases)
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Every stoichiometry calculation is the same journey: turn the data you are given into an amount in moles, cross the balanced equation using its coefficients, then turn the answer back into the mass, volume or concentration the question asks for.
> The only real decisions are which reactant runs out first and which substances are gases.

## Key ideas
**Amount in moles** is the common currency. Three routes in, the same three routes out:

$$n = \frac{m}{M_r} \qquad n = cV \qquad n = \frac{V}{24.0}$$

- From a **mass**: $n = m/M_r$, with $A_r$ and $M_r$ values from the Periodic Table printed in the paper.
- From a **solution**: $n = cV$ with $c$ in $\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ and $V$ in $\mathrm{dm^3}$. A volume in $\mathrm{cm^3}$ must be divided by 1000 first.
- From a **gas at r.t.p.**: $n = V/24.0$, because one mole of *any* gas occupies $24.0\ \mathrm{dm^3\,mol^{-1}}$ at r.t.p., whatever its $M_r$. Equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules, so **volumes of gases may be used directly as mole ratios**.

**Limiting reagent:** the reactant used up first. It is the one with the smallest value of (amount in moles ÷ its coefficient in the balanced equation). Every product amount is calculated from it. Any other reactant is **in excess**.

**Percentage yield** compares what was made with the most that could have been made:

$$\text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual amount of product}}{\text{theoretical amount of product}} \times 100$$

The theoretical amount always comes from the limiting reagent.

## Method
1. Write and balance the equation, with state symbols.
2. Convert every piece of given data into moles ($m/M_r$, $cV$, or $V/24.0$).
3. If two reactant amounts are given, divide each by its coefficient and pick the smallest: that is the limiting reagent.
4. Multiply by the ratio (coefficient of wanted ÷ coefficient of limiting reagent) to get the amount of the substance asked about.
5. Convert back: $m = nM_r$, $V = n \times 24.0\ \mathrm{dm^3}$, or $c = n/V$.
6. Round at the end, to the number of significant figures the data give or the question asks for.

> [!example]- Worked example
> 3.00 g of calcium carbonate is added to 40.0 cm$^3$ of 1.00 mol dm$^{-3}$ hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume of carbon dioxide produced at r.t.p.
>
> $$\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}$$
>
> $n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 3.00/100.1 = 0.02997\ \mathrm{mol}$ and $n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.0400 \times 1.00 = 0.0400\ \mathrm{mol}$.
>
> Divide by coefficients: $0.02997/1 = 0.02997$ against $0.0400/2 = 0.0200$. The **acid** is limiting, even though there is more of it in moles.
>
> $n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.0400/2 = 0.0200\ \mathrm{mol}$, so
>
> $$V = 0.0200 \times 24.0 = 0.480\ \mathrm{dm^3}$$

## Traps
- **Trap:** the reactant with fewer moles must be limiting. **Why it's wrong:** it ignores the coefficients; a reactant needed 2:1 runs out twice as fast. **Instead:** always divide the amount by the coefficient before comparing.
- **Trap:** equal masses mean equal amounts, so equal volumes of gas. **Why it's wrong:** $n = m/M_r$, so the lighter formula gives more moles. **Instead:** convert each mass separately; $5.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of Ca is $0.12\ \mathrm{mol}$ but $5.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of Rb is only $0.058\ \mathrm{mol}$.
- **Trap:** percentage yield $=$ mass of product ÷ mass of reactant. **Why it's wrong:** these are different substances, so their masses are not comparable. **Instead:** find the theoretical mass of product first, then divide.
- **Trap:** counting the water in a "final volume of gas at room conditions". **Why it's wrong:** water is a liquid at room conditions and condenses out. **Instead:** count only leftover gaseous reactants and gaseous products — but do include steam when the stated temperature is above $100\ \mathrm{^\circ C}$.
- **Trap:** using $c = n/V$ with $V$ in $\mathrm{cm^3}$. **Why it's wrong:** concentration is defined per $\mathrm{dm^3}$. **Instead:** divide any $\mathrm{cm^3}$ value by 1000 before it enters a formula.

## Exam technique
*Calculate* means show the working: a mark is usually available for the amounts in moles even if the final answer slips. *Deduce* and *Determine* questions give you data and expect you to work backwards to a formula, a ratio or an identity — compare your computed value with each option rather than guessing. *Show (that)* needs every step written down. Quote answers to the significant figures the data justify, neither fewer nor more, and never round partway through a multi-step calculation. In a titration question, scale the titre to the whole volumetric flask before comparing with the mass weighed out.
