# 3.6 Intermolecular forces, electronegativity and bond properties
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Electron sharing, molecular shape and attractions between molecules determine whether a substance has a permanent dipole and how readily its molecules separate. Water is unusual because its molecules form an extensive hydrogen-bond network.

## Key ideas
Electronegativity is an atom's ability to attract the bonding pair of electrons. When bonded atoms have different electronegativities, electron density shifts towards the more electronegative atom. That atom becomes $\delta^-$ and the other $\delta^+$: the bond is polar. A molecule has an overall dipole moment only if its bond dipoles do not cancel. Linear $\ce{CO2}$ has polar C–O bonds but no overall dipole; bent $\ce{H2O}$ has a permanent dipole.

Van der Waals forces is a generic term for intermolecular forces between molecular entities other than those due to bond formation. Every molecule has instantaneous dipole-induced dipole (id-id), or London dispersion, forces. Electron movement creates a fleeting uneven charge distribution in one molecule. This induces a dipole in a neighbour, and opposite partial charges attract. More electrons and a more polarisable cloud generally strengthen these forces: from $\ce{Cl2}$ to $\ce{Br2}$ to $\ce{I2}$, volatility decreases as the forces between molecules increase.

Polar molecules additionally experience permanent dipole-permanent dipole (pd-pd) attractions: the positive end of one molecule attracts the negative end of another. Hydrogen bonding is a particularly strong case of pd-pd attraction. At this level, identify it between molecules with N–H or O–H groups: the $\delta^+$ hydrogen bonded to N or O is attracted to a lone pair on N or O in another molecule. Water and ammonia are simple examples. An O atom without an O–H bond may accept a hydrogen bond from water, but a sample of such molecules cannot necessarily hydrogen-bond to itself.

Water's extensive hydrogen bonding explains its anomalously high melting and boiling points compared with similar small hydrides: extra energy is needed to separate water molecules. Strong cohesion between molecules at the surface gives water relatively high surface tension. In ice, hydrogen bonds hold water molecules in an open lattice. The molecules occupy more volume than in liquid water, where some bonds break and molecules can move closer together. Therefore solid ice is less dense than liquid water and floats.

In general, ionic, covalent and metallic bonding are stronger than intermolecular forces. When a simple molecular substance boils, attractions **between** molecules are overcome; covalent bonds **within** each molecule remain. Melting giant covalent silicon dioxide is different because bonds throughout its network must be broken.

## Method
1. Identify the particles: molecules, ions or a giant network. For molecules, check whether the molecule has N–H or O–H groups.
2. Decide whether its bond dipoles cancel using molecular shape. Assign London forces to every molecular substance; add pd-pd forces for polar molecules and hydrogen bonding where applicable.
3. To explain a melting or boiling comparison, name the force **between molecules**, compare its strength and then link that strength to energy needed or volatility. Do not use the energy of a bond within an intact molecule to explain boiling.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Explain why $\ce{H2O}$ has a higher boiling point than $\ce{H2S}$.
> Oxygen is sufficiently electronegative for O–H groups in neighbouring water molecules to hydrogen-bond. Sulfur hydride does not form significant hydrogen bonds. More energy is therefore needed to separate water molecules on boiling.

## Traps
- **Trap:** A polar bond always means a polar molecule. **Why it's wrong:** Symmetrical bond dipoles can cancel. **Instead:** Check shape and add the dipoles as vectors.
- **Trap:** Boiling breaks covalent bonds. **Why it's wrong:** Molecules remain intact on boiling. **Instead:** Name the intermolecular forces overcome.
- **Trap:** Non-polar means no attraction. **Why it's wrong:** Temporary dipoles occur in every electron cloud. **Instead:** Include London dispersion forces for all molecules.
- **Trap:** Ice is denser because it is ordered. **Why it's wrong:** Its hydrogen-bonded arrangement is open. **Instead:** Link the larger volume per molecule to lower density.

## Exam technique
For **describe**, give the interacting atoms and partial charges. For **explain**, connect the named force to the observed property, such as boiling point or surface tension. When comparing substances with similar electron counts, test whether one has an additional permanent dipole. State clearly whether the force is within or between molecules.
