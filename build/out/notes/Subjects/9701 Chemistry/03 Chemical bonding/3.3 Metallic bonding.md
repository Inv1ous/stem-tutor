# 3.3 Metallic bonding
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A metal is a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalised electrons. Metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged particles.

## Key ideas
A metal contains a regular, giant lattice of **positive metal ions**. Some outer-shell electrons are **delocalised**: they are not assigned to one atom or to one bond between two atoms. They can move throughout the structure. The ions and electrons have opposite charges, so they attract one another.

The precise definition to learn is: **metallic bonding is the electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and delocalised electrons**. Both particles and the type of force matter. If a question asks you to *define* metallic bonding, include all three parts. A vague answer such as “the attraction between atoms” does not identify the particles responsible.

The term *lattice* means a repeating arrangement. In a simple diagram, draw repeated circles for positive metal ions and small dots among them for delocalised electrons. Label both explicitly. A circle carrying only a plus sign might be read as an unspecified positive particle; write “positive metal ion”. Likewise, a dot or minus sign should be labelled “delocalised electron”. The diagram is a two-dimensional view of a three-dimensional structure. The electrons are spread through the lattice, not trapped in the spaces shown by the dots.

## Method
1. Identify the metal ions as positive, rather than neutral atoms.
2. Identify the outer electrons as delocalised across the lattice.
3. State that the force between them is electrostatic attraction.
4. If asked to explain a property, connect the particle model to that property rather than repeating only the definition.

> [!example]- Worked example
> **Question:** Explain why copper has a high melting point in terms of its metallic structure.
> **Step 1:** Copper forms a giant lattice of positive metal ions surrounded by delocalised electrons.
> **Step 2:** There is strong electrostatic attraction between these ions and electrons.
> **Step 3:** A large amount of energy is needed to overcome enough of this attraction for the lattice to melt. The explanation concerns ions and delocalised electrons, not bonds between neutral copper atoms.

The same particle model explains electrical conduction: delocalised electrons can move through the structure and carry charge. If a question asks about conductivity, explicitly mention their ability to move. If it asks about melting point, refer to energy needed to overcome the attraction. These are applications of the model; the definition itself remains the electrostatic attraction stated above.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Describing attraction between cations and anions. **Why it's wrong:** A pure metal does not have a lattice of negative ions. **Instead:** Name delocalised electrons as the negative particles.
- **Trap:** Describing neutral metal atoms attracting one another. **Why it's wrong:** The outer electrons are delocalised, leaving positive ions at the lattice sites. **Instead:** Label those sites as positive metal ions.
- **Trap:** Drawing shared electron pairs between neighbours. **Why it's wrong:** A fixed shared pair describes covalent bonding. **Instead:** Show and label delocalised electrons throughout the lattice.

## Exam technique
For *define*, give the precise definition in one sentence. For *describe* or *sketch*, identify the giant arrangement and label both kinds of particle. For *explain*, make a causal link: state the charged particles and attraction, then connect it to the property named in the question. In a comparison of metals, do not claim a different bonding type; discuss how the strength of metallic attraction differs when the given evidence supports it.
