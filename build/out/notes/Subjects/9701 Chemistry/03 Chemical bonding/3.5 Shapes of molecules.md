# 3.5 Shapes of molecules
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Electron regions around a central atom repel and arrange themselves as far apart as possible. Count lone pairs as regions when predicting the arrangement, but name molecular shape from the positions of atoms.

## Key ideas
VSEPR means **valence shell electron pair repulsion** theory. Bonding pairs and lone pairs around a central atom repel each other. A single, double or triple bond counts as **one region** of electron density for this purpose. Lone pairs repel bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel one another, so they compress bond angles.

With no central lone pairs, two regions give a **linear** molecule, as in $\ce{CO2}$: $180°$. Three give **trigonal planar**, as in $\ce{BF3}$: $120°$. Four give **tetrahedral**, as in $\ce{CH4}$: $109.5°$. Five give **trigonal bipyramidal**, as in $\ce{PF5}$: equatorial–equatorial bonds make $120°$ and axial–equatorial bonds make $90°$. Six give **octahedral**, as in $\ce{SF6}$: adjacent bonds make $90°$, while opposite bonds make $180°$.

Four regions need not give a tetrahedral *molecular* shape. In $\ce{NH3}$, three bonding pairs and one lone pair put the three H atoms in a **pyramidal** shape; the H–N–H angle is $107°$. In $\ce{H2O}$, two bonding pairs and two lone pairs leave a **non-linear** molecule with an H–O–H angle of $104.5°$. Both have a roughly tetrahedral electron-region arrangement, but molecular shape names ignore lone-pair positions.

## Method
1. Draw or infer the central atom's bonds and lone pairs, checking the charge of an ion. Treat each multiple bond as one bonding region.
2. Count all regions and choose the arrangement with maximum separation: two linear, three trigonal planar, four tetrahedral, five trigonal bipyramidal, or six octahedral.
3. Name the shape using only the bonded atoms. Use the matching ideal angle, then consider whether lone-pair repulsion reduces it.
4. For an analogous species, compare its bond-pair and lone-pair counts with one of the syllabus examples. State both shape and bond angle when asked.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Predict the shape and H–N–H angle of $\ce{NH4+}$, and compare it with $\ce{NH3}$.
> Nitrogen in $\ce{NH4+}$ has four N–H bonds and no lone pair after accepting a proton. Its four bonding regions are tetrahedral, so H–N–H is $109.5°$.
> Nitrogen in $\ce{NH3}$ has three bonds and one lone pair. Its molecular shape is pyramidal and its H–N–H angle is $107°$, compressed by stronger lone-pair–bond-pair repulsion.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling $\ce{NH3}$ tetrahedral. **Why it's wrong:** That describes all four electron regions, including the lone pair. **Instead:** Name the three atom positions: pyramidal.
- **Trap:** Counting a double bond twice. **Why it's wrong:** VSEPR counts regions, not bond order. **Instead:** Count each multiple bond once; $\ce{CO2}$ has two regions and is linear.
- **Trap:** Saying octahedral $\ce{SF6}$ has only $90°$ angles. **Why it's wrong:** Opposite F atoms lie across S. **Instead:** Include the $180°$ trans angle where relevant.

## Exam technique
*State* questions need the shape and the specified angle. *Explain* questions need the bond-pair and lone-pair count, the repulsion comparison, and the resulting shape or angle. *Predict* questions reward identifying which familiar VSEPR pattern an unfamiliar ion matches. For example, $\ce{BH4-}$ matches $\ce{CH4}$ (tetrahedral, $109.5°$), $\ce{H3O+}$ matches $\ce{NH3}$ (pyramidal, about $107°$), and $\ce{SCl2}$ matches $\ce{H2O}$ (non-linear, about $104.5°$). With $\ce{SO2}$, two S–O bonding regions and a lone pair give a non-linear molecule; do not assume its angle equals water's because it has three regions rather than four.
