# 3.1 Electronegativity and bonding
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Electronegativity tells you how powerfully an atom attracts electrons to itself. Its pattern across the Periodic Table helps you predict whether two elements will share electrons in a covalent bond or form ions with ionic bonding.

## Key ideas
**Electronegativity** is *the power of an atom to attract electrons to itself*. Pauling values express this as a relative scale: a higher value means stronger attraction. The definition concerns an atom's attraction for electrons. It is different from first ionisation energy, which concerns removing an electron from a gaseous atom.

Three factors control electronegativity. **Nuclear charge** is the positive charge of the nucleus: more protons strengthen the attraction for electrons. **Atomic radius** describes the size of the atom; a larger radius places electrons farther from the nucleus, reducing attraction. **Shielding** by electrons in inner shells and sub-shells reduces the attraction felt by electrons farther out. These factors act together, so a greater nuclear charge alone does not guarantee greater electronegativity.

Across a period from left to right, electronegativity generally **increases**. Nuclear charge rises as proton number rises, while added electrons enter the same principal shell, so shielding by inner shells changes little. Atomic radius decreases, strengthening attraction. For example, chlorine is more electronegative than sodium in Period 3.

Down a group, electronegativity generally **decreases**. Each step adds an occupied principal shell, increasing atomic radius and inner-shell shielding. Although nuclear charge also rises, greater distance and shielding outweigh its effect on the attraction of electrons. Thus fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine, and chlorine more electronegative than bromine.

## Method
1. Read the two **Pauling electronegativity** values supplied in the question. Calculate the **electronegativity difference** as the larger value minus the smaller value, $\Delta\chi = |\chi_A-\chi_B|$. The difference cannot be negative.
2. Compare the attractions. A small difference means the atoms attract electrons similarly, favouring **covalent bonding** through sharing. A large difference means attraction is strongly unequal, favouring electron transfer and **ionic bonding** between oppositely charged ions.
3. When asked to compare two candidate bonds, calculate both differences and choose the larger one as more likely ionic. Use the values supplied; do not impose an exact universal cut-off unless the question gives one. The presence of covalent character in an ionic compound is outside this syllabus outcome.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Sodium has Pauling electronegativity 0.9 and chlorine 3.2. Predict the bond type.
> Calculate the magnitude of the difference:
> $$\Delta\chi = |3.2-0.9| = 2.3$$
> This is a large difference, so electron transfer is favoured. Predict **ionic bonding** in sodium chloride.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling electronegativity the ease of losing an electron. **Why it's wrong:** That confuses attraction with removal. **Instead:** State the precise definition: power of an atom to attract electrons to itself.
- **Trap:** Saying shielding stays constant down a group. **Why it's wrong:** More inner shells are occupied. **Instead:** Explain both the increased radius and the increased shielding.
- **Trap:** Deciding bond type from one element's value. **Why it's wrong:** Bond type depends on the difference between the two values. **Instead:** Subtract before predicting.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, use the exact definition. For **Explain**, name the factor, state how it changes, then link it to attraction for electrons. For **Determine** or **Predict** bonding, show the absolute difference, describe it as small or large relative to the alternatives, and give the bond type. Do not use ionisation energy exceptions to claim the electronegativity trend has identical dips.
