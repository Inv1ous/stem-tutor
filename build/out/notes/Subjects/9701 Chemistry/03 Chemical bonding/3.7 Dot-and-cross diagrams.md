# 3.7 Dot-and-cross diagrams
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A dot-and-cross diagram tracks the outer-shell electrons supplied by different atoms. It makes electron transfer, ordinary shared pairs and donor-supplied coordinate pairs visible.

## Key ideas
A **dot-and-cross diagram** uses two symbols to show where valence electrons came from. The symbols are bookkeeping: a dot electron and a cross electron have identical physical properties. Draw only outer-shell electrons unless a question asks otherwise. Put a pair between atoms for a covalent bond and pairs not used in bonds beside their atom as lone pairs.

In **ionic bonding**, electrons transfer and the resulting ions attract electrostatically. Draw each ion separately in square brackets with its charge. For $\ce{MgO}$, magnesium loses two electrons and oxygen gains two: $[\ce{Mg}]^{2+}$ and $[\ce{O}]^{2-}$. Oxygen's eight outer electrons should include its original six and the two transferred electrons. For $\ce{Mg2Si}$, two Mg atoms each lose two electrons, so the ions are two $\ce{Mg^{2+}}$ and one $\ce{Si^{4-}}$. Do not join them with shared pairs.

In **covalent bonding**, atoms share electron pairs. In $\ce{NH3}$, nitrogen makes three N–H shared pairs and retains one lone pair. In $\ce{CO2}$, each C=O double bond contains two shared pairs; each oxygen has two lone pairs. A triple bond contains three shared pairs.

A **coordinate (dative covalent) bond** is a shared pair in which both electrons were supplied by one atom. The nitrogen lone pair in $\ce{NH3}$ is donated to $\ce{H+}$ to make $\ce{NH4+}$. Draw four N–H pairs inside brackets labelled +; distinguish the originally donated pair by using two nitrogen symbols. Once formed, the four N–H bonds are equivalent. In $\ce{CO}$, the customary dot-and-cross account has two ordinary shared pairs and one pair donated from O to C. In the $\ce{Al2Cl6}$ dimer, bridging chlorines donate lone pairs towards electron-deficient aluminium atoms.

The octet is a useful check, not an absolute rule. Boron in $\ce{BF3}$ has six surrounding electrons. Phosphorus in $\ce{PCl5}$ has an expanded octet of ten. Neutral $\ce{NO}$ has eleven valence electrons in total, so at least one is unpaired. These cases can appear in questions.

## Method
1. Count valence electrons from every atom. Add electrons for a negative charge or subtract for a positive charge.
2. Decide whether the structure contains separate ions or bonded atoms. For ions, show brackets and charges before placing electrons.
3. Place bonding pairs and then remaining lone or unpaired electrons, preserving dots and crosses to show their origins.
4. Count the electrons again. Check each H has two, typical second-period atoms have eight where appropriate, and the total charge is correct.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Draw $\ce{NH4+}$ formed from $\ce{NH3}$ and $\ce{H+}$.
> Count $5+4(1)-1=8$ valence electrons. Three original N–H bonds use six; nitrogen's original lone pair supplies the fourth N–H bond to $\ce{H+}$. Put four bonding pairs around N in brackets marked +. There are no lone pairs on N and eight electrons around it.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Join the ions in $\ce{MgO}$ with a covalent shared pair. **Why it's wrong:** Mg transfers two electrons to O. **Instead:** Show separately charged bracketed ions.
- **Trap:** Show a C→O coordinate pair in $\ce{CO}$. **Why it's wrong:** The conventional diagram credits oxygen as donor. **Instead:** Show O→C for the coordinate pair.
- **Trap:** Force every atom into an octet. **Why it's wrong:** Electron-deficient, expanded-octet and odd-electron species exist. **Instead:** Check the total count first.

## Exam technique
For **Sketch** or **Demonstrate**, make every outer electron and charge countable. For **Explain**, state which atom supplies the pair and which accepts it. Label an unusual coordinate pair clearly; a plain line alone hides its origin.
