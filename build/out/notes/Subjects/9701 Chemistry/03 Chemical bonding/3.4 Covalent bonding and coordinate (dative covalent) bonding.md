# 3.4 Covalent bonding and coordinate (dative covalent) bonding
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A covalent bond is the electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons. Orbital overlap splits every bond into one $\sigma$ bond plus a $\pi$ bond for each extra shared pair, and the shorter the bond, the stronger it is.

## Key ideas
**Covalent bonding** is the electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons. One shared pair gives a single bond ($\ce{H2}$, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{HCl}$, $\ce{CH4}$, $\ce{C2H6}$), two give a double bond ($\ce{O2}$, $\ce{C2H4}$, and two of them in $\ce{CO2}$), three give a triple bond ($\ce{N2}$). Ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$, has three bonding pairs and one lone pair on nitrogen.

Period 3 elements can **expand the octet**: 10 outer electrons on the sulfur of $\ce{SO2}$ and the phosphorus of $\ce{PCl5}$, 12 on the sulfur of $\ce{SF6}$. Each shared pair counts two electrons for *both* bonded atoms.

In a **coordinate (dative covalent) bond** both electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom: a lone pair is donated into an empty orbital on the acceptor. When ammonia and hydrogen chloride gases meet, the nitrogen lone pair is donated to $\ce{H+}$, giving $\ce{NH4+}$ with one coordinate bond — after which all four $\ce{N-H}$ bonds are identical. In $\ce{Al2Cl6}$, each $\ce{AlCl3}$ unit donates a chlorine lone pair to the aluminium atom of the other, so there are two coordinate bonds.

A **$\sigma$ bond** is formed by direct, end-on overlap of orbitals between the bonding atoms. A **$\pi$ bond** is formed by the sideways overlap of adjacent p orbitals, above and below the $\sigma$ bond. So single $=1\sigma$; double $=1\sigma+1\pi$; triple $=1\sigma+2\pi$. $\ce{H2}$ is one $\sigma$ bond from 1s overlap; $\ce{C2H6}$ has 7 $\sigma$ bonds; $\ce{C2H4}$ has 5 $\sigma$ and 1 $\pi$; $\ce{HCN}$ has 2 $\sigma$ and 2 $\pi$; $\ce{N2}$ has 1 $\sigma$ and 2 $\pi$.

**Hybridisation** mixes one s with some p orbitals to make equivalent orbitals for $\sigma$ bonding: four $\sigma$ bonds means $sp^3$ (tetrahedral, $\ce{C2H6}$), three means $sp^2$ (planar, $120°$, $\ce{C2H4}$), two means sp (linear, $\ce{HCN}$). Unhybridised p orbitals are left for the $\pi$ bonds.

**Bond energy** is the energy required to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state; **bond length** is the internuclear distance of two covalently bonded atoms.

![[Assets/9701/9701-3.4-bond-length-energy.svg]]

More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer, so the bond is shorter and stronger: $\ce{C-C}$ 154 pm, 350 kJ mol$^{-1}$; $\ce{C=C}$ 134 pm, 610 kJ mol$^{-1}$; $\ce{C#C}$ 120 pm, 840 kJ mol$^{-1}$. A stronger bond is harder to break, so the molecule is less reactive: $\ce{N2}$ (994 kJ mol$^{-1}$) is inert at room temperature while $\ce{O2}$ (496 kJ mol$^{-1}$) reacts readily, and $\ce{I2}$ has the longest, weakest halogen bond and dissociates most easily.

## Method
1. **Counting bonds in a molecule.** Write the full structure, hydrogen atoms included. Count every pair of bonded atoms: that is the number of $\sigma$ bonds. Then add one $\pi$ bond per double bond and two per triple bond.
2. **Hybridisation at an atom.** Count its $\sigma$ bonds: 4 gives $sp^3$, 3 gives $sp^2$, 2 gives sp.
3. **Coordinate bonds.** Look for a donor with a lone pair next to an acceptor with an empty orbital ($\ce{H+}$, $\ce{Al}$ in $\ce{AlCl3}$); each such donation is one coordinate bond.
4. **Bond energy sums.** Moles of bonds $=$ moles of substance $\times$ bonds of that type per molecule; multiply by the bond energy and quote the answer in kJ.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Determine the number of $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds in $\ce{CH3CH=CH-C#CH}$.
> The chain of 5 carbon atoms gives 4 carbon–carbon bonds, and $\ce{C5H6}$ gives 6 carbon–hydrogen bonds.
> Every bond between two atoms contains one $\sigma$ bond: $4+6=10$ $\sigma$ bonds.
> Extra pairs: the double bond adds 1 $\pi$ and the triple bond adds 2 $\pi$, so 3 $\pi$ bonds.
> Answer: 10 $\sigma$ and 3 $\pi$. The two triply bonded carbon atoms are sp hybridised.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Defining a covalent bond as "a shared pair of electrons". **Why it's wrong:** The mark needs the force. **Instead:** "The electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons."
- **Trap:** Calling a triple bond three $\pi$ bonds, or ignoring $\ce{C-H}$ bonds when counting $\sigma$ bonds. **Why it's wrong:** Each bonded pair of atoms has exactly one $\sigma$ bond. **Instead:** Triple $=1\sigma+2\pi$; count every $\ce{C-H}$ bond as a $\sigma$ bond.
- **Trap:** Saying a longer bond is stronger. **Why it's wrong:** A larger internuclear distance weakens the attraction. **Instead:** Shorter bond, higher bond energy, lower reactivity.
- **Trap:** Calling bond energy the energy released when a bond forms. **Why it's wrong:** It is defined for bond breaking. **Instead:** Energy required to break one mole of the bond in the gaseous state.

## Exam technique
*Define* needs the exact wording above, including "gaseous state" for bond energy and "internuclear" for bond length. *Describe* the bonding in a molecule by naming the type of bond, the number of shared pairs and any lone pairs. *Explain* questions on reactivity must chain the reasoning: number of shared pairs → bond length → bond energy → how easily the bond breaks. For $\sigma$/$\pi$ counting, write the displayed formula out before counting; the marks are lost to missed $\ce{C-H}$ bonds, not to the chemistry.
