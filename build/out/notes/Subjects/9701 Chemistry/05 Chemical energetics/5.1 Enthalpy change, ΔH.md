# 5.1 Enthalpy change, $\Delta H$

> [!abstract] In one breath
> A reaction transfers energy as bonds break and form. The enthalpy change tells you whether energy is released or absorbed overall, per amount of reaction specified by the equation.

## Key ideas

The enthalpy change is $\Delta H=H_{\mathrm{products}}-H_{\mathrm{reactants}}$. In an **exothermic** reaction, energy transfers to the surroundings, products lie lower in enthalpy, and $\Delta H<0$. In an **endothermic** reaction, energy is taken in, products lie higher, and $\Delta H>0$. A warm heat pad signals an exothermic process. Do not assign a positive sign because the surroundings warmed: their gain is the reacting system's loss.

A reaction pathway diagram plots enthalpy vertically against progress of reaction horizontally. The curve rises to a maximum before falling to the product level. The forward **activation energy**, $E_a$, is the gap from reactants to that maximum; $\Delta H$ is the gap from reactants to products, with its sign. For the reverse reaction, measure activation energy from products to the same peak. For example, with reactants at $20$, products at $50$ and peak at $100\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, the forward reaction has $E_a=80\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, the reverse has $E_a=50\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and $\Delta H=+30\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.

![[Assets/9701/9701-5.1-exothermic.svg]]

The standard symbol $\ominus$ means measurements at **298 K and 101 kPa** in this syllabus. A substance's **standard state** is its most stable physical form under those conditions. The standard enthalpy change of reaction, $\Delta H_r^\ominus$, is for the stoichiometric amounts in the equation, with substances in standard states. The standard enthalpy change of formation, $\Delta H_f^\ominus$, forms **one mole** of a compound from its constituent elements in their standard states. For instance, graphite, rather than gaseous carbon atoms, is the standard form of carbon. An element in its standard state has $\Delta H_f^\ominus=0$ by definition.

The standard enthalpy change of combustion, $\Delta H_c^\ominus$, is for **one mole** of a substance completely burned in oxygen. The standard enthalpy change of neutralisation, $\Delta H_{neut}^\ominus$, is when an aqueous acid and aqueous alkali form **one mole of water**. If the balanced equation forms two moles of water, divide its reaction enthalpy by two to quote neutralisation enthalpy.

## Method

1. **Bond energies:** Draw or inspect the molecules and count bonds changed using the balanced equation. Breaking a bond absorbs energy; forming a bond releases it. Use $\Delta H_r\approx\sum E(\mathrm{bonds\ broken})-\sum E(\mathrm{bonds\ formed})$. A tabulated *average* bond energy is a mean over bonds of the same type in different gaseous molecules. An *exact* bond dissociation enthalpy refers to a specified bond-breaking process in a specified gaseous species. The estimate may differ from an experimental enthalpy because the molecular environments differ.
2. **Calorimetry:** Find the mass $m$ of water or solution being heated, its temperature change $\Delta T$, and its specific heat capacity $c$. Calculate $q=mc\Delta T$ in joules. Find the moles $n$ of the reactant specified by the question, then $\Delta H=-q/n$, converting joules to kilojoules when reporting $\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. A temperature rise gives a negative reaction enthalpy.

> [!example]- Worked example
> In $\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}$, bond energies are H–H $436$, Cl–Cl $242$ and H–Cl $431\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.
> Broken bonds require $436+242=678\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.
> Two H–Cl bonds form and release $2(431)=862\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$.
> $$\Delta H_r\approx678-862=-184\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$$
> The negative sign is consistent with the larger energy release on bond formation.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Label the peak-to-product gap as forward activation energy. **Why it's wrong:** The forward reaction starts at reactants. **Instead:** Measure its barrier from the reactant level.
- **Trap:** Reverse the bond-energy subtraction. **Why it's wrong:** Breaking absorbs and forming releases. **Instead:** Subtract the formed-bond total from the broken-bond total.
- **Trap:** Put the fuel mass into $q=mc\Delta T$. **Why it's wrong:** The measured temperature rise is for the water or solution. **Instead:** Use that heated mass, then divide by moles of fuel or other specified reactant.

## Exam technique

For **define**, include the one-mole basis, standard states, and standard conditions where relevant. For **calculate**, show the counted bonds or the $q$ substitution, use the correct mole denominator, state the sign, and give units. A temperature *difference* has the same numerical value in kelvin and degrees Celsius; do not add 273 to a temperature rise.
