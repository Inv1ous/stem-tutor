# 5.2 Hess’s law
> [!abstract] In one breath
> The enthalpy change between two chemical states is independent of the route taken. A cycle lets us combine measured enthalpy changes to find one that is difficult to measure directly.

## Key ideas
**Hess’s law:** the enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided initial and final conditions are the same. Enthalpy is a state function. This means every path from the same reactants in the same physical states to the same products in the same physical states has the same total $\Delta H$. Temperature and pressure must also match.

An **enthalpy cycle** draws two routes between common endpoints. Label each arrow with an equation and its signed enthalpy change. Reversing an arrow changes the sign; multiplying an equation by a number multiplies $\Delta H$ by that number. Add changes along a route in the direction travelled.

For standard enthalpies of formation, start at the elements in their standard states. Form each substance in the balanced reaction. The useful result is

$$
\Delta H_r^\ominus=\sum\Delta H_f^\ominus(\text{products})-\sum\Delta H_f^\ominus(\text{reactants}).
$$

Each term includes the coefficient in the equation. An element in its standard state, such as $\ce{O2(g)}$, has $\Delta H_f^\ominus=0$.

For a combustion cycle, burn reactants and products to the **same final substances**, such as $\ce{CO2(g)}$ and $\ce{H2O(l)}$. The direct reaction plus combustion of its products equals combustion of its reactants. Match the number of carbon and hydrogen atoms and the water state before adding enthalpies.

Bond energy data provide another indirect route for gaseous species:

$$
\Delta H\approx\sum E(\text{bonds broken})-\sum E(\text{bonds formed}).
$$

Breaking bonds requires energy; making bonds releases it. Count bonds from the balanced equation. Average bond energies usually make the result an estimate, and a liquid or solid must first be converted to gas if its bonds are treated this way.

## Method
1. Write and balance the target equation. Mark physical states, the desired direction, and whether $\Delta H$ is per mole of reaction as written.
2. Choose common endpoints. For formation data, use elements in standard states; for combustion data, use common complete combustion products.
3. Draw or list both routes and label every arrow with its balanced equation and enthalpy change.
4. Reverse and scale supplied equations until unwanted species cancel. Reverse the sign whenever you reverse an equation; scale the enthalpy whenever you scale the equation.
5. Add the signed changes and check that the resulting equation is exactly the target. State $\Delta H$ in kJ mol$^{-1}$ and interpret its sign.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Find $\Delta H$ for $\ce{2NO(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)}$ using $\Delta H_f^\ominus(\ce{NO})=+90$ and $\Delta H_f^\ominus(\ce{NO2})=+33$ kJ mol$^{-1}$.
> The common starting point is $\ce{N2(g) + 2O2(g)}$. Forming two NO molecules contributes $2(+90)$; forming two NO₂ molecules contributes $2(+33)$.
> $$
> \Delta H_r^\ominus=2(33)-[2(90)+0]=-114\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}.
> $$
> Oxygen is an element in its standard state, so its formation enthalpy is zero. The negative result means the stated reaction is exothermic.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Keep the same sign when going backwards around a cycle. **Why it's wrong:** the reverse process undoes the energy change. **Instead:** change $+q$ to $-q$ or vice versa when reversing an arrow.
- **Trap:** Add unscaled tabulated formation values. **Why it's wrong:** a value is for one mole of substance, while the equation may contain several. **Instead:** multiply each value by its coefficient first.
- **Trap:** Use bond energies for a liquid as though it were gaseous. **Why it's wrong:** bond energy data refer to gaseous species. **Instead:** account for vaporisation in the cycle or use enthalpy data suited to the stated physical states.

## Exam technique
*Calculate* calls for a visible equation, substitution with signed values, and a final unit. In a longer *show that* question, demonstrate how the supplied equations sum to the target; an unexplained collection of bond energies often conceals a sign or coefficient error. When an arrow is unclear, write the chemical equation next to it. If the final sign seems surprising, compare product and reactant formation totals before changing it.
