# 11.3 Some reactions of the halide ions
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Halide ions become stronger reducing agents down Group 17 because they lose electrons more readily. Their reactions with silver ions and concentrated sulfuric acid reveal both their identity and their reducing ability.

## Key ideas
A **reducing agent** donates electrons and is itself oxidised. The halide-ion reducing-strength order is $\ce{F- < Cl- < Br- < I-}$. This is the reverse of the halogens' oxidising-strength order. For example, $\ce{2I- -> I2 + 2e-}$. Iodide reduces bromine, whereas bromide cannot reduce iodine.

With aqueous silver nitrate, the halide ions form insoluble silver halides:

$$\ce{Ag+(aq) + X-(aq) -> AgX(s)}$$

The observations form a useful sequence. $\ce{AgCl}$ is **white** and dissolves in **dilute** aqueous ammonia. $\ce{AgBr}$ is **cream** and dissolves in **concentrated** aqueous ammonia, but not dilute ammonia. $\ce{AgI}$ is **yellow** and remains insoluble even in concentrated ammonia. Ammonia removes silver ions into a soluble complex, allowing some silver halide to dissolve; the complex formula is not required here. Acidify the sample with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate, so other anions are less likely to give misleading precipitates.

Concentrated sulfuric acid first acts as an acid with a solid halide. For sodium chloride:

$$\ce{NaCl(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HCl(g)}$$

The steamy white hydrogen chloride fumes do not mean chlorine has formed: chloride is too weak a reducing agent to reduce the acid under these conditions. Bromide first forms hydrogen bromide by the analogous acid reaction, then reduces sulfuric acid:

$$\ce{2HBr(g) + H2SO4(l) -> Br2(g) + SO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}$$

This gives brown bromine and sulfur dioxide. Iodide first forms hydrogen iodide, then is oxidised to iodine. As a stronger reducing agent, it can reduce sulfuric acid to sulfur dioxide, sulfur, or hydrogen sulfide. Balanced examples are:

$$\ce{2HI + H2SO4 -> I2 + SO2 + 2H2O}$$
$$\ce{6HI + H2SO4 -> 3I2 + S + 4H2O}$$
$$\ce{8HI + H2SO4 -> 4I2 + H2S + 4H2O}$$

## Method
1. For a silver-ion test, identify the precipitate colour first, then report what dilute and concentrated ammonia do.
2. For concentrated sulfuric acid, write the acid reaction that forms the hydrogen halide. Decide whether a further redox reaction occurs.
3. Check redox equations by balancing atoms and confirming that halide oxidation matches sulfur reduction.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A dry sample of sodium bromide is treated separately with aqueous silver nitrate and with concentrated sulfuric acid.
> Silver nitrate gives a cream precipitate: $\ce{Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)}$. It does not dissolve in dilute ammonia but dissolves in concentrated ammonia.
> Sulfuric acid first releases $\ce{HBr}$: $\ce{NaBr + H2SO4 -> NaHSO4 + HBr}$.
> Then $\ce{2HBr + H2SO4 -> Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O}$. Bromide loses electrons; sulfur changes oxidation number from $+6$ to $+4$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating all silver halides as soluble in ammonia. **Why it's wrong:** Their solubilities differ greatly. **Instead:** Match dilute ammonia to AgCl, concentrated ammonia to AgBr, and neither to AgI.
- **Trap:** Calling HCl fumes chlorine. **Why it's wrong:** The chloride reaction is proton transfer without oxidation. **Instead:** Look for a halogen product only when the halide reduces sulfuric acid.
- **Trap:** Calling bromide the oxidising agent when bromine forms. **Why it's wrong:** Bromide loses electrons. **Instead:** Name bromide as the reducing agent.

## Exam technique
For **describe**, give the exact colour, solubility and reagent concentration. For **explain**, state which substance loses electrons and which is reduced. For **give** an equation, balance atoms and charge and include state symbols when the reaction conditions justify them. Keep the acid step and the subsequent redox step distinct.
