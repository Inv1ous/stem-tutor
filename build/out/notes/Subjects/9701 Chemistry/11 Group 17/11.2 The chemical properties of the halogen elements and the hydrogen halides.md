# 11.2 The chemical properties of the halogen elements and the hydrogen halides
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Halogens become weaker oxidising agents down Group 17, and their reactions with hydrogen generally become less vigorous. Hydrogen halides become less thermally stable down the group because their H–X bonds weaken.

## Key ideas
A halogen molecule, $\ce{X2}$, acts as an **oxidising agent** when it accepts electrons to form halide ions: $\ce{X2 + 2e- -> 2X-}$. Oxidising power decreases in the order $\ce{F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2}$. A halogen higher in this order oxidises the halide ions of a halogen below it. Thus chlorine displaces bromide and iodide, bromine displaces iodide, and iodine displaces neither chloride nor bromide. For example, $\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq)}$. The halogen is reduced while the halide ion is oxidised. Do not confuse the reactivity of a halogen molecule with the reducing power of its halide ion: halide reducing power rises from fluoride to iodide.

Each halogen combines with hydrogen to form a hydrogen halide: $\ce{H2(g) + X2(g) -> 2HX(g)}$. Fluorine reacts explosively, even in the dark. Chlorine reacts slowly in darkness but rapidly or explosively in light. Bromine reacts with hydrogen on heating, less vigorously than chlorine. Iodine reacts slowly and reversibly on heating. The overall tendency toward less vigorous reaction down the group is consistent with decreasing halogen oxidising power: accepting electrons becomes less favourable. Reaction conditions matter, so quote the light or heat where relevant.

**Thermal stability** is resistance to decomposition on heating. It decreases $\ce{HF > HCl > HBr > HI}$. Down Group 17, the halogen atom gets larger, so the H–X covalent bond becomes longer and weaker. Less energy is needed to break the bond, and the hydrogen halide decomposes more readily. For instance, hydrogen iodide can decompose to hydrogen and iodine on heating, whereas hydrogen bromide is more resistant under the same conditions. This trend concerns the covalent bond **within** each molecule, not the intermolecular forces between molecules.

## Method
1. For a displacement prediction, place both halogens in the oxidising-power order. The higher halogen can oxidise the lower halide; write the new halide and the displaced halogen.
2. Balance the ionic equation by transferring two electrons: $\ce{X2 + 2Y- -> 2X- + Y2}$, provided X is above Y in the order.
3. For thermal stability, compare H–X bond strengths. Name the stronger bond, then say which hydrogen halide requires more energy to decompose.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Predict the reaction when bromine is added to aqueous potassium iodide and explain it.
> Bromine is above iodine in oxidising power, so bromine oxidises iodide ions.
> $\ce{Br2(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> 2Br-(aq) + I2(aq)}$.
> Bromine is reduced and iodide is oxidised. Potassium ions are spectators.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Iodine displaces bromide because both are halogens. **Why it's wrong:** Iodine is below bromine in oxidising power. **Instead:** Compare their positions before writing products.
- **Trap:** A purple hexane layer proves iodine was formed by displacement. **Why it's wrong:** Iodine initially added also dissolves in hexane. **Instead:** Count iodine already present as well as iodine produced.
- **Trap:** HI is the most thermally stable because iodine is largest. **Why it's wrong:** The H–I bond is the weakest H–X bond. **Instead:** Link stronger bonds to greater thermal stability.

## Exam technique
For **describe**, give the order and the observable or reaction conditions. For **explain**, link the trend explicitly to oxidising power or H–X bond strength. In displacement equations, include diatomic halogen molecules and balance both atoms and charge. If a question uses two solvent layers, identify which layer contains dissolved iodine before selecting its colour.
