# 11.1 Physical properties of the Group 17 elements
> [!abstract] In one breath
> From chlorine to iodine, the elements become less volatile because attractions between their molecules grow stronger. This intermolecular trend is distinct from the weakening covalent bond within each molecule.

## Key ideas
At room temperature, chlorine is a pale green gas, bromine is a red-brown liquid and iodine is a grey-black solid. The vapour of iodine is purple, but the solid is grey-black. A volatile substance **evaporates readily**. Volatility decreases in the order chlorine, bromine, iodine: chlorine escapes into the gas phase most readily and iodine least readily. The melting and boiling points rise in the same order.

Each element consists of diatomic molecules, $\ce{Cl2}$, $\ce{Br2}$ or $\ce{I2}$. Inside each molecule, the atoms share a covalent bond. From Cl–Cl through Br–Br to I–I, atomic size and bond length increase. The bonding orbitals overlap less effectively, so the covalent bond becomes weaker. Keep this **bond strength trend** separate from the volatility trend: boiling and evaporation separate molecules; they do not normally break the X–X bond.

The molecules are non-polar because each contains two identical atoms. Their intermolecular attractions are **instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces** (London dispersion forces). Electron clouds fluctuate, giving a temporary dipole that induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule. Down the group, each molecule has more electrons and a larger, more polarisable electron cloud. The temporary dipoles and the attractions they cause become stronger. More energy is needed to separate molecules, so boiling point increases and volatility decreases.

## Method
1. State the observed direction: from chlorine to iodine, volatility decreases.
2. Name the relevant particles and force: instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces **between molecules**.
3. Explain the cause: increasing electron number makes the electron cloud more polarisable, strengthening those forces.
4. Link the force to the observation: more energy is required to separate molecules, making evaporation less easy.

> [!example]- Worked example
> **Explain why bromine is less volatile than chlorine.** Bromine molecules have more electrons than chlorine molecules. Their electron clouds are more polarisable, so the instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces between bromine molecules are stronger. More energy is needed to separate bromine molecules, so bromine evaporates less readily.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Use the Br–Br or Cl–Cl covalent bond energy to explain evaporation. **Why it's wrong:** evaporation separates intact molecules. **Instead:** compare forces between molecules.
- **Trap:** Say these molecules attract through permanent dipoles. **Why it's wrong:** each $\ce{X2}$ molecule has identical atoms and no permanent dipole. **Instead:** name instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces.
- **Trap:** Say volatility rises with boiling point. **Why it's wrong:** a higher boiling point indicates that more energy is needed to separate molecules. **Instead:** higher boiling point goes with lower volatility.

## Exam technique
For **Describe**, give the trend or the three colours and states without replacing it with an explanation. For **Explain**, give the complete chain: more electrons, greater polarisability, stronger induced dipole forces **between molecules**, then less ready evaporation. Saying only “van der Waals forces increase” can be too vague: identify the specific force and the particles it acts between. When asked about **bond strength**, specify the covalent X–X bond inside a molecule and explain its increasing length and less effective overlap.
