# 17.1 Aldehydes and ketones
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Aldehydes and ketones contain a carbonyl group, but their different positions in the molecule change how they oxidise and respond to tests. The carbonyl bond also accepts nucleophilic attack, so both classes can be reduced or converted into hydroxynitriles.

## Key ideas
An **aldehyde** has a terminal $\ce{-CHO}$ group; a **ketone** has $\ce{RCO-R'}$, with the carbonyl carbon bonded to two carbon groups. To prepare an aldehyde from a **primary alcohol**, heat with acidified $\ce{K2Cr2O7}$ or acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ and **distil** the aldehyde as it forms. For example, $\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}$. Keeping the aldehyde in hot oxidant under reflux would oxidise it further to a carboxylic acid. Oxidising a **secondary alcohol** with either acidified oxidant, then distilling, produces a ketone: $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH3 + H2O}$. Under these usual conditions, a ketone resists further oxidation.

Reduction runs in the other direction. $\ce{NaBH4}$ or $\ce{LiAlH4}$ reduces an aldehyde to a **primary alcohol** and a ketone to a **secondary alcohol**. The carbonyl carbon gains hydrogen while the oxygen becomes an OH group. Thus ethanal gives ethanol and propanone gives propan-2-ol.

With $\ce{HCN}$, $\ce{KCN}$ as catalyst and heat, either carbonyl forms a **hydroxynitrile**. Ethanal gives $\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}$, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile; propanone gives $\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CN}$, 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile. The nitrile carbon adds one carbon atom to the parent skeleton.

## Method
1. For the **nucleophilic addition mechanism**, show $\ce{CN^-}$ attacking the partially positive carbonyl carbon. Draw a curly arrow from the cyanide carbon lone pair to that carbon and another from the $\ce{C=O}$ π bond to oxygen. The intermediate is an alkoxide.
2. Show the alkoxide oxygen taking a proton from $\ce{HCN}$; the $\ce{H-CN}$ bond electrons return to carbon to regenerate $\ce{CN^-}$. The final carbon bears both OH and CN.
3. For an **unknown**, first add 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH): an orange or yellow-orange precipitate indicates an aldehyde or ketone carbonyl. Then warm a fresh sample with Tollens’ reagent or Fehling’s solution. A silver mirror with Tollens’ or a brick-red precipitate with Fehling’s identifies an aldehyde. A ketone gives neither response. Aldehydes are also readily oxidised by acidified dichromate(VI), while ketones are not under these conditions.
4. Test for $\ce{CH3CO-}$ with warm **alkaline $\ce{I2(aq)}$**. A yellow precipitate of tri-iodomethane, $\ce{CHI3}$, shows a positive iodoform test. For $\ce{CH3CO-R}$, the other organic product is $\ce{RCO2^-}$. Ethanal and methyl ketones such as propanone are positive; pentan-3-one is negative.

> [!example]- Worked example
> An unknown gives orange crystals with 2,4-DNPH, no silver mirror with Tollens’ reagent, and yellow solid with alkaline iodine. Deduce its class and key group.
> 2,4-DNPH confirms an aldehyde or ketone carbonyl. The negative Tollens’ test points to a ketone. Yellow $\ce{CHI3}$ means it contains $\ce{CH3CO-}$: it is a methyl ketone. Propanone is one possible example, but the observations do not determine a unique structure.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Reflux a primary alcohol and expect only an aldehyde. **Why it's wrong:** The aldehyde oxidises further. **Instead:** Distil it promptly.
- **Trap:** Treat an orange 2,4-DNPH precipitate as proof of an aldehyde. **Why it's wrong:** Ketones also react. **Instead:** Use Tollens’ or Fehling’s reagent next.
- **Trap:** Assume every ketone gives iodoform. **Why it's wrong:** The carbonyl needs a methyl neighbour. **Instead:** Look specifically for $\ce{CH3CO-}$.

## Exam technique
For **Describe**, name the reagent, conditions and observation or organic product. For **Deduce**, tie each test result to a functional group before naming a compound. In a mechanism, the first curly arrow must start at a lone pair on cyanide carbon; the second starts at the C=O π bond. Count the extra carbon from CN when naming a hydroxynitrile.
