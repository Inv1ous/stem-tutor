# 15.1 Halogenoalkanes
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A halogenoalkane has a carbon–halogen bond that can be replaced by a nucleophile or eliminated to form an alkene. Its class and C–X bond strength help predict mechanism and rate.

## Key ideas
A **primary** halogenoalkane has one carbon attached to the C–X carbon; a **secondary** one has two; a **tertiary** one has three. Count neighbours of the carbon *bearing* X, not the position number in the name. For example, $\ce{CH3CHBrCH3}$ is secondary.

There are three preparation families. An alkane such as ethane reacts with $\ce{Cl2}$ or $\ce{Br2}$ by free-radical substitution under ultraviolet light: $\ce{C2H6 + Cl2 -> C2H5Cl + HCl}$. An alkene undergoes electrophilic addition of $\ce{X2}$ or $\ce{HX(g)}$ at room temperature; $\ce{X2}$ adds two halogen atoms across C=C, whereas HX adds one halogen and one hydrogen. An alcohol can undergo substitution with $\ce{HX(g)}$; $\ce{KCl}$ with concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ or concentrated $\ce{H3PO4}$; $\ce{PCl3}$ and heat; $\ce{PCl5}$; or $\ce{SOCl2}$. These reagent lists are worth learning exactly.

The polar C–X bond makes its carbon susceptible to nucleophilic attack. Heat a halogenoalkane with **aqueous** $\ce{NaOH}$ to form an alcohol. Heat it with $\ce{KCN}$ in ethanol to form a nitrile: the carbon end of $\ce{CN^-}$ bonds to the chain, so its carbon count rises by one. Heat with $\ce{NH3}$ in ethanol under pressure to form an amine. In contrast, **hot ethanolic** $\ce{NaOH}$ removes H from an adjacent carbon and X from the reaction carbon: elimination forms C=C. Bromoethane therefore gives ethene with hot ethanolic hydroxide but ethanol with aqueous hydroxide.

In $S_N2$, the nucleophile attacks the C–X carbon while C–X breaks in the same step. Draw the arrow from the nucleophile's lone pair to that carbon and the second arrow from the C–X bond to X. There is no separate carbocation. In $S_N1$, C–X first breaks heterolytically to form a carbocation; the nucleophile then attacks it. Alkyl groups donate electron density by their positive inductive effect and stabilise a positive carbon. Tertiary carbocations are most stable, but a tertiary reaction carbon is crowded and hinders $S_N2$ attack. Thus primary halogenoalkanes tend to $S_N2$, tertiary ones to $S_N1$, and secondary ones can use a mixture depending on structure.

## Method
1. Identify the reagent, solvent and temperature. Aqueous hydroxide suggests substitution; hot ethanolic hydroxide suggests elimination.
2. Count carbon neighbours of the C–X carbon to classify the substrate and predict $S_N1$ or $S_N2$ preference.
3. For substitution, replace X with OH, CN or an amino group. For elimination, remove X and a hydrogen on an adjacent carbon, then draw C=C. Check that the adjacent carbon actually has a hydrogen.
4. For the silver nitrate test, warm the compound with aqueous $\ce{AgNO3}$ in ethanol. Hydrolysis releases halide ions; $\ce{Ag^+}$ gives white $\ce{AgCl}$, cream $\ce{AgBr}$ or yellow $\ce{AgI}$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Predict the main products of bromoethane with aqueous and hot ethanolic sodium hydroxide.
> Aqueous $\ce{OH^-}$ replaces Br: $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH^- -> CH3CH2OH + Br^-}$. The product is ethanol.
> Hot ethanolic hydroxide removes Br and an adjacent H: $\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH^- -> CH2=CH2 + H2O + Br^-}$. The product is ethene.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Assuming chlorine compounds hydrolyse fastest because C–Cl is most polar. **Why it's wrong:** C–Cl is stronger than C–Br and C–I. **Instead:** For comparable structures, rate increases from chloro- to bromo- to iodoalkane as C–X strength decreases.
- **Trap:** Drawing a carbocation in $S_N2$. **Why it's wrong:** Bond making and bond breaking are simultaneous. **Instead:** Draw one concerted step.
- **Trap:** Calling the silver nitrate colour the colour of the organic product. **Why it's wrong:** The solid is a silver halide. **Instead:** Link white, cream and yellow to $\ce{AgCl}$, $\ce{AgBr}$ and $\ce{AgI}$.

## Exam technique
For **describe**, state both reagent and conditions as well as product. For **explain** rate trends, explicitly connect weaker C–X bonds to easier bond breaking and earlier precipitate formation. Show electron-pair arrows from a bond or lone pair, never from an atom label.
