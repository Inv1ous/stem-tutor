# 14.2 Alkenes
> [!abstract] In one breath
> The electron-rich $\ce{C=C}$ bond makes alkenes undergo addition and oxidation. Conditions determine whether oxidation adds two OH groups or breaks the carbon skeleton; carbocation stability determines the main product of unsymmetrical electrophilic addition.

## Key ideas
An alkene contains a carbon–carbon double bond. Its π electrons are available to an electrophile. Alkenes are prepared by **elimination** of $\ce{HX}$ from a halogenoalkane using **ethanolic $\ce{NaOH}$ and heat**. **Dehydration** removes $\ce{H2O}$ from an alcohol, using heated $\ce{Al2O3}$ catalyst or concentrated $\ce{H2SO4}$ with heat. **Cracking** a longer-chain alkane also yields shorter molecules, including alkenes. For example, $\ce{CH3CH2Br -> CH2=CH2}$ loses $\ce{HBr}$ under elimination conditions.

The alkene π bond undergoes electrophilic addition. $\ce{H2(g)}$ with Pt or Ni catalyst and heat hydrogenates it to an alkane. Steam, $\ce{H2O(g)}$, with $\ce{H3PO4}$ catalyst hydrates it to an alcohol. $\ce{HX(g)}$ adds at room temperature to form a halogenoalkane, while $\ce{X2}$ gives a dihalogenoalkane. For instance, $\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> BrCH2CH2Br}$. These are addition reactions because atoms join without loss of a small molecule.

Cold, dilute, acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ oxidises $\ce{C=C}$ to a **diol**, putting one OH on each double-bond carbon. Hot, concentrated, acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ **ruptures** the double bond. A double-bond carbon bearing H is oxidised further to a carboxylic acid; a carbon with no H forms a ketone. A terminal $\ce{CH2=}$ carbon can ultimately form $\ce{CO2}$. Locate the original double bond by pairing the carbonyl carbons of the fragments. Many ethene or propene molecules also join by **addition polymerisation**: their double bonds open to make poly(ethene) or poly(propene), with no small-molecule by-product.

## Method
1. For a reaction, identify the starting functional group and exact reagent conditions before choosing addition, elimination, or oxidation.
2. For the bromine water test, shake the sample with aqueous bromine. Rapid change from orange to colourless indicates a $\ce{C=C}$ bond: bromine has added across it.
3. For an electrophilic addition mechanism, draw a curly arrow **from the π bond** to the electrophile. Draw the bond-electron arrow to the leaving atom, then show attack by the resulting nucleophile on the carbocation. Each arrow starts at an electron pair.
4. For an unsymmetrical alkene, draw both possible carbocations and compare them. Alkyl groups donate electron density by the **inductive effect**: tertiary carbocation > secondary > primary in stability. The route through the more stable cation gives the major product, explaining Markovnikov addition.

> [!example]- Worked example
> **Predict the major product of $\ce{HBr}$ with propene.** The π bond attacks H in $\ce{HBr}$; the $\ce{H-Br}$ bond pair moves to Br. Protonation at the end carbon gives the secondary $\ce{CH3CH+CH3}$ carbocation. Protonation at the middle carbon would give a less stable primary cation. $\ce{Br-}$ attacks the secondary cation, so the major product is 2-bromopropane.

For $\ce{Br2}$ with ethene, the π bond polarises and attacks one Br; the $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond breaks towards the other Br. Bromide then attacks the positively charged intermediate. Show the arrows from the π bond and from the $\ce{Br-Br}$ bond, followed by the lone pair on $\ce{Br-}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating every acidified $\ce{KMnO4}$ reaction as cleavage. **Why it's wrong:** Cold dilute reagent gives a diol. **Instead:** Read temperature and concentration first.
- **Trap:** Starting a curly arrow at H or Br. **Why it's wrong:** Arrows show electron-pair movement. **Instead:** Start the first arrow at $\ce{C=C}$.
- **Trap:** Explaining the major product by a stable bromoalkane. **Why it's wrong:** Regioselectivity arises earlier. **Instead:** Compare intermediate carbocation stabilities.

## Exam technique
For **Describe**, give reagents, conditions and organic products. For **Explain** Markovnikov addition, name the inductive effect, compare both carbocations and link the more stable one to the major product. For **Deduce** oxidative fragments, mark each original double-bond carbon and whether it bears H before assigning acid or ketone products.
