# 13.2 Characteristic organic reactions
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Classify an organic reaction by what changes in the molecules, then classify its mechanism by how electrons move. A full curly arrow follows an electron pair from a bond or lone pair to its destination.

## Key ideas
A **homologous series** is a family with the same functional group and general formula; successive members differ by $\ce{CH2}$. A **saturated** organic compound contains only carbon–carbon single bonds. An **unsaturated** compound contains a carbon–carbon double or triple bond.

In **homolytic fission**, each bonded atom receives one electron, forming **free radicals**, species with an unpaired electron. In **heterolytic fission**, one atom receives both bonding electrons, forming ions. During a radical chain reaction, **initiation** forms radicals, **propagation** uses a radical and produces another, and **termination** combines radicals to remove them.

A **nucleophile** donates an electron pair; an **electrophile** accepts one. These may be ions or neutral molecules. **Addition** joins reactants to give one product, often across a multiple bond. **Substitution** replaces an atom or group. **Elimination** removes atoms or groups, often forming a multiple bond. **Hydrolysis** breaks a bond by reaction with water. **Condensation** joins molecules with loss of a small molecule, commonly water.

In organic chemistry, **oxidation** can mean gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen; **reduction** can mean loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen. In equations, $[O]$ represents one oxygen atom supplied by an oxidising agent; $[H]$ represents one hydrogen atom supplied by a reducing agent.

## Method
1. Identify what enters, leaves or changes: this gives addition, substitution, elimination, hydrolysis, condensation or redox.
2. Track the attacking species. A nucleophile donates a lone pair to an electron-poor centre; an electrophile accepts a pair from an electron-rich bond or lone pair.
3. For a full curly arrow, begin at the bond or lone pair supplying **two** electrons and end where the pair goes. Show relevant bond polarity and charges.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Classify the reaction of a ketone with $\ce{HCN}$ and describe the key arrows.
> The $\ce{C=O}$ bond is polar: carbon is $\delta^+$ and oxygen $\delta^-$. The carbon end of $\ce{CN^-}$ supplies a lone pair to the carbonyl carbon. At the same time, the $\ce{C=O}$ electron pair moves to oxygen. The resulting alkoxide is protonated to give a hydroxynitrile. This is **nucleophilic addition** because cyanide adds across the carbonyl group.

The four mechanism names combine the attacker and the overall change. **Free-radical substitution** replaces an atom through a radical chain, as in alkane chlorination under UV light. **Electrophilic addition** adds an electrophile across an alkene $\ce{C=C}$ bond; its π electrons first attack the electrophile. **Nucleophilic substitution** replaces a leaving group, as when $\ce{CN^-}$ replaces bromide in a halogenoalkane. **Nucleophilic addition** adds a nucleophile across a carbonyl $\ce{C=O}$ bond.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating homolysis as ion formation. **Why it's wrong:** each atom takes one bonding electron. **Instead:** draw radicals with unpaired electrons.
- **Trap:** Starting a curly arrow at the atom being attacked. **Why it's wrong:** that atom is the destination, not the electron source. **Instead:** start at a bond or lone pair.
- **Trap:** Assuming every nucleophile is negatively charged. **Why it's wrong:** neutral molecules such as ammonia donate lone pairs. **Instead:** test for an available electron pair.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, give the electron-pair wording precisely. For **Describe** a mechanism, include charges or polarity and place each arrow's tail at the electron pair; in nucleophilic addition, do not invent a positive carbonyl intermediate. Use $[O]$ and $[H]$ only with their stated one-atom meanings.
