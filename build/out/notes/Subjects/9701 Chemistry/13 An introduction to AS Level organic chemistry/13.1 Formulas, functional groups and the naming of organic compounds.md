# 13.1 Formulas, functional groups and the naming of organic compounds
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A hydrocarbon is made up of carbon and hydrogen atoms only. A functional group gives an organic family its characteristic properties; formulas and systematic names describe its atoms and connectivity.

## Key ideas
A **hydrocarbon** is a compound made up of carbon and hydrogen atoms only. Ethene qualifies; ethanol does not, because it also contains oxygen. **Alkanes** are simple saturated hydrocarbons with no functional group. Their carbon atoms are joined by single bonds. A functional group is the atom or group of atoms responsible for a compound's characteristic chemical reactions. It dictates chemical properties and strongly influences physical properties across a homologous series. An alkene has a C=C group; an alcohol has –OH; an aldehyde has –CHO; a ketone has C=O within a chain; a carboxylic acid has –COOH. Other syllabus families include halogenoalkanes, esters, acyl chlorides, amines, amides and nitriles. The same carbon skeleton with a different group can behave very differently.

A **general formula** describes every member of a homologous series using $n$: for acyclic alkanes it is $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}$, and for monohydric saturated alcohols it is $\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}O}$. A **structural formula** shows how atoms and groups are connected, such as $\ce{CH3CH2OH}$. A **displayed formula** shows every atom and every bond, including the O–H bond; writing a condensed structural formula when a displayed formula is requested can lose a mark. A **skeletal formula** uses an unlabelled line end or vertex for each carbon. Carbon-bound hydrogens are omitted: infer enough to give each neutral carbon four bonds. Heteroatoms and hydrogens attached to them are shown. These representations can describe the same molecule; they reveal different amounts of detail.

## Method
1. **Interpret a formula:** Count all labelled atoms and every skeletal line end and vertex. Complete each carbon's valency of four, including multiple bonds. Count hydrogen on O or N when shown. This gives the **molecular formula**, the actual number of each atom in one molecule.
2. **Find an empirical formula:** Divide every molecular subscript by their highest common factor. The empirical formula is the simplest whole-number atom ratio. For $\ce{C4H8O2}$, divide by two to get $\ce{C2H4O}$; do not reduce only some elements.
3. **Name a simple aliphatic molecule:** Choose the longest parent chain containing the main functional group. Use meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent- or hex- for one to six carbons. Number from the end that gives the principal functional group the lowest possible locant; then locate double bonds and substituents. State locants, prefixes and the appropriate suffix, such as -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid or -nitrile. The nitrile carbon counts in the parent chain. For an ester $\ce{RCOOR'}$, name the alkyl group $\ce{R'}$ attached to oxygen first, then the acid-derived alkanoate $\ce{RCOO}$; the carbonyl carbon belongs to the latter chain. Use straight chains for esters and nitriles in this syllabus, with up to six carbons in each ester part.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Name $\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}$ and deduce its molecular and empirical formulas.
> The group beyond the single-bond oxygen is methyl; the acid-derived chain including the carbonyl carbon has three carbons. The systematic name is **methyl propanoate**.
> Count four carbons, eight hydrogens and two oxygens: molecular formula $\ce{C4H8O2}$. Divide subscripts by two: empirical formula $\ce{C2H4O}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treat the empirical formula as the molecular formula. **Why it's wrong:** A simplest ratio may represent several molecular sizes. **Instead:** Count actual atoms first, then reduce only for an empirical formula.
- **Trap:** Count only corners and miss line ends in skeletal formulas. **Why it's wrong:** Both stand for carbons. **Instead:** Mark every end and vertex before adding hydrogens.
- **Trap:** Reverse ester parts. **Why it's wrong:** The alkyl name comes from the group bonded to the single-bond oxygen. **Instead:** Find $\ce{-COO-}$ before naming either part.

## Exam technique
*Define* hydrocarbon with “carbon and hydrogen atoms only.” In *deduce* questions, show how skeletal valencies give the molecular formula and how the common factor gives the empirical formula. When asked for a displayed formula, draw every bond, especially O–H. Check the parent chain and locants before finalising a systematic name.
