# 9.2 Periodicity of chemical properties of the elements in Period 3
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Across Period 3, electronegativity rises and bonding in compounds changes from mostly ionic to covalent. The oxides change from basic through amphoteric to acidic, while chloride solutions become more acidic.

## Key ideas

### Why the trends occur
1. Across Period 3, nuclear charge increases while shielding remains similar because added electrons enter the same main shell. Attraction for bonding electrons increases, so electronegativity rises.
2. The electronegativity difference from oxygen or chlorine generally decreases: oxide and chloride bonding becomes more covalent across the period.
3. Ionic metal oxides contain basic $\ce{O^2-}$ ions that accept protons. Sodium oxide reacts readily with water; magnesium oxide reacts only slightly and leaves a suspension. $\ce{Al2O3}$ is amphoteric: it reacts with acids and aqueous NaOH despite being insoluble in water.
4. Molecular phosphorus and sulfur oxides react with water to form acids and neutralise bases. Giant covalent $\ce{SiO2}$ is an important exception to any claim that all covalent oxides react with water. For chlorides, NaCl dissolves without hydrolysis, hydrated aluminium ions acidify water, and molecular $\ce{SiCl4}$ and $\ce{PCl5}$ hydrolyse. Bonding predicts broad trends, but structure and reaction conditions still matter.

Sodium, magnesium, aluminium, phosphorus and sulfur react with oxygen to give the specified oxides:
$\ce{4Na + O2 -> 2Na2O}$; $\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}$; $\ce{4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3}$; $\ce{P4 + 5O2 -> P4O10}$; $\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}$. Sodium burns yellow and sulfur blue. With chlorine: $\ce{2Na + Cl2 -> 2NaCl}$, $\ce{Mg + Cl2 -> MgCl2}$, $\ce{2Al + 3Cl2 -> 2AlCl3}$, $\ce{Si + 2Cl2 -> SiCl4}$ and $\ce{P4 + 10Cl2 -> 4PCl5}$. Sodium reacts vigorously with cold water: $\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}$. Magnesium reacts only slowly with cold water: $\ce{Mg + 2H2O -> Mg(OH)2 + H2}$; with steam it forms $\ce{MgO}$ and $\ce{H2}$.

The highest oxidation number rises with the number of valence electrons: +1 in $\ce{Na2O}$ and $\ce{NaCl}$; +2 in $\ce{MgO}$ and $\ce{MgCl2}$; +3 in $\ce{Al2O3}$ and $\ce{AlCl3}$; +4 in $\ce{SiCl4}$; and +5 in $\ce{P4O10}$ and $\ce{PCl5}$. Sulfur is +4 in $\ce{SO2}$ and +6 in $\ce{SO3}$. Assign oxygen −2 and chlorine −1, then make the oxidation numbers sum to zero.

## Method
1. For an oxide added to water, decide whether it reacts. $\ce{Na2O + H2O -> 2NaOH}$ gives a strongly alkaline solution (pH about 13–14). $\ce{MgO + H2O -> Mg(OH)2}$ gives a sparingly soluble weakly alkaline suspension (pH about 9–10). $\ce{Al2O3}$ and $\ce{SiO2}$ do not react. $\ce{P4O10 + 6H2O -> 4H3PO4}$, $\ce{SO2 + H2O <=> H2SO3}$ and $\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}$ give acidic solutions (typically pH 1–3, depending on concentration).
2. Classify acid–base behaviour. $\ce{Na2O}$, $\ce{MgO}$, $\ce{NaOH}$ and $\ce{Mg(OH)2}$ are basic: they neutralise acids. $\ce{P4O10}$, $\ce{SO2}$ and $\ce{SO3}$ are acidic: they react with aqueous NaOH. $\ce{Al2O3}$ and $\ce{Al(OH)3}$ are amphoteric: they react with both acids and NaOH. For example, $\ce{Al2O3 + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2O}$ and $\ce{Al2O3 + 2NaOH + 3H2O -> 2Na[Al(OH)4]}$; $\ce{Al(OH)3 + 3HCl -> AlCl3 + 3H2O}$ and $\ce{Al(OH)3 + NaOH -> Na[Al(OH)4]}$. A basic example is $\ce{MgO + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + H2O}$; an acidic one is $\ce{SO2 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO3 + H2O}$.
3. For a chloride added to water, distinguish dissolution from hydrolysis. $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$ gives pH about 7. Hydrated $\ce{Mg^{2+}}$ releases some $\ce{H+}$, so $\ce{MgCl2}$ is slightly acidic (pH about 6). $\ce{AlCl3}$ forms acidic hydrated aluminium ions: $\ce{AlCl3 + 6H2O -> [Al(H2O)6]^3+ + 3Cl-}$, followed by $\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+ + H2O <=> [Al(H2O)5(OH)]^2+ + H3O+}$. $\ce{SiCl4 + 2H2O -> SiO2 + 4HCl}$ gives white solid and acidic fumes; $\ce{PCl5 + 4H2O -> H3PO4 + 5HCl}$ gives a strongly acidic solution. Their pH is usually about 1–3, depending on concentration.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Explain why $\ce{SiO2}$ and $\ce{SiCl4}$ have different melting points and responses to water.
> $\ce{SiO2}$ has a giant covalent lattice with strong Si–O bonds throughout and does not react with water.
> $\ce{SiCl4}$ has simple molecules with weak intermolecular forces, so it is volatile; water hydrolyses its Si–Cl bonds to make $\ce{SiO2}$ and $\ce{HCl}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling amphoteric oxides neutral. **Why it's wrong:** $\ce{Al2O3}$ reacts with both acid and aqueous NaOH. **Instead:** Give an equation for each reaction.
- **Trap:** Calling $\ce{SiCl4}$ giant covalent because silicon is. **Why it's wrong:** The chloride has discrete molecules. **Instead:** Infer bonding from melting point, conductivity and hydrolysis together.
- **Trap:** Calling $\ce{AlCl3}$ solution neutral. **Why it's wrong:** Hydrated aluminium ions hydrolyse. **Instead:** State an acidic pH.

## Exam technique
Use *describe* for observations and equations, *explain* for a causal link to electronegativity and bonding, and *suggest* when inferring bonding from property evidence. Balance atoms and charges, and say whether a pH refers to a solution or a suspension. A high melting point alone does not prove ionic bonding: $\ce{SiO2}$ is giant covalent.


> [!example]- Worked contrast: dissolution or hydrolysis?
> **NaCl:** $\ce{NaCl(s) -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}$. Water hydrates existing ions; no hydrolysis produces NaOH or HCl. There is one Na and one Cl on each side, and the total charge is zero. The solution is approximately pH 7.
> **Silicon chloride:** $\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}$. Hydrolysis gives a white silica solid in an acidic aqueous phase; steamy fumes may appear during addition. Each side contains one Si, four Cl, four H and two O atoms.
> The white solid is not dissolved silica. Dissolved HCl forms $\ce{H3O+(aq)}$, which causes acidity; chloride ions alone do not make NaCl solution acidic. Use (s) for silica and (aq) for HCl retained in excess water.
