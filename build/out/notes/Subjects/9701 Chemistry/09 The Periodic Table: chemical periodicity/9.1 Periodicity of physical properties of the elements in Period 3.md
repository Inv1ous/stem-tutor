# 9.1 Periodicity of physical properties of the elements in Period 3
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Across sodium to argon, atomic and ionic sizes, melting point and electrical conductivity change in recognisable patterns. Explain each pattern using nuclear attraction, structure and bonding rather than memorising a single rising or falling line.

## Key ideas

**Atomic radius.** From sodium towards chlorine, atomic radius decreases. Proton number and nuclear charge increase, while added electrons enter the same main shell and shielding by inner shells stays approximately constant. Stronger attraction draws the outer shell closer. Argon is often omitted from comparisons of covalent atomic radii because it does not normally form covalent bonds.

**Ionic radius.** Do not draw one smooth trend through every common ion. Na⁺, Mg²⁺ and Al³⁺ are isoelectronic: increasing nuclear charge makes their radii decrease. There is a large jump to the anions P³⁻, S²⁻ and Cl⁻, whose electrons occupy the third shell. These three ions are also isoelectronic; their radii decrease as nuclear charge increases. The common ion of silicon is not normally included in this simple comparison.

**Melting point.** Sodium, magnesium and aluminium are metals. Their melting points rise because the metallic bonds strengthen: cation charge increases, cation radius decreases and the number of delocalised electrons per atom increases. Silicon has the highest melting point: it is a giant covalent lattice with many strong Si–Si bonds to break. Phosphorus exists as P₄ molecules, sulfur as S₈ molecules and chlorine as Cl₂ molecules. Melting these substances overcomes weak instantaneous dipole–induced dipole attractions between molecules, not the covalent bonds within them. Sulfur melts above phosphorus because larger S₈ molecules have more electrons and stronger intermolecular attractions. Chlorine melts lower; monatomic argon has the lowest melting point.

**Electrical conductivity.** Na, Mg and Al conduct well because mobile delocalised electrons carry charge through their metallic lattices. Aluminium generally conducts best of the three. Silicon is a semiconductor: its conductivity is far below that of the metals, although it can increase with temperature. P, S, Cl and Ar lack mobile charged particles and do not conduct electricity in their elemental forms.

## Method

1. Place the elements in Period 3 order: Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, Ar.
2. For a radius comparison, check whether the particles are atoms, cations or anions. Compare isoelectronic ions within their own series.
3. For melting or conductivity, identify the structure first: metallic, giant covalent, simple molecular or monatomic.
4. Name the attraction overcome on melting, or the mobile charge carrier for conductivity. Link its strength or availability to the observed property.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Explain why silicon has a much higher melting point than sulfur.
> Silicon has a giant covalent lattice. Melting it requires many strong Si–Si covalent bonds to be broken.
> Sulfur consists of S₈ molecules. Melting sulfur overcomes much weaker instantaneous dipole–induced dipole attractions between molecules; the S–S bonds within each molecule remain intact.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Ionic radius decreases smoothly from Na⁺ to Cl⁻. **Why it's wrong:** The cations lose the third shell, while the anions retain it. **Instead:** Describe the two decreasing isoelectronic series and the jump between them.
- **Trap:** Silicon has strong intermolecular forces. **Why it's wrong:** Its atoms form one giant covalent network. **Instead:** Name the Si–Si covalent bonds that must be broken.
- **Trap:** Silicon conducts like aluminium because it has four outer electrons. **Why it's wrong:** Those electrons participate in covalent bonds. **Instead:** Contrast a semiconductor with mobile delocalised electrons in a metal.

## Exam technique

For **describe**, give the pattern including its peak, jump or reversal. For **explain**, give the structure, the relevant particles or bonds, and the causal link to the property. When comparing silicon and sulfur, identify exactly which attractions are overcome on melting.
