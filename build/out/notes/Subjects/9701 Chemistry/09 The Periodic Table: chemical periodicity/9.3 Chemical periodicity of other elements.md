# 9.3 Chemical periodicity of other elements
> [!abstract] In one breath
> An element's position in the Periodic Table gives a basis for predicting its characteristic properties. To identify an unknown, combine several physical and chemical clues, then check that the proposed element fits every one.

## Key ideas
Elements in one group have the same number of outer-shell electrons, which explains many similarities in chemical properties. A measured trend among known group members can be extended cautiously to predict a member for which data are not supplied. A prediction should state both the direction of a property change and the reason for it.

Down Group 2, increasing distance and shielding make outer electrons easier to remove. First and second ionisation energies generally decrease, and reaction with water generally becomes more vigorous. Group 2 hydroxides generally become more soluble down the group, while carbonates become more thermally stable. Thus an element below barium is predicted to react faster with water and form a more soluble hydroxide and more thermally stable carbonate than barium. These are predictions from trends, not direct observations of every possible element.

Across Period 3, atomic radius generally decreases from sodium to chlorine because nuclear charge rises while electrons are added to the same principal shell. Sodium has the largest atomic radius and magnesium the second largest. Three occupied electron shells place an atom in Period 3. The number of outer electrons then helps establish its group.

Chemical behaviour supplies further clues. Magnesium and aluminium ions can each give a white hydroxide precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Aluminium hydroxide dissolves in excess aqueous sodium hydroxide; magnesium hydroxide does not. Silicon forms covalent silicon tetrachloride and needs strong heating to react with oxygen. The Period 3 chlorides and oxides therefore help narrow an unknown's identity, but one clue alone may be shared by several elements.

## Method
1. Extract each observation separately. Mark whether it indicates a period, group, physical trend, or chemical reaction.
2. Use electron shells or an atomic-radius ranking to narrow the period. Use outer electrons and known group behaviour to narrow the group.
3. Test each candidate against every observation. Eliminate a candidate as soon as one definite clue contradicts it.
4. State the possible position and identity, then justify them with the most discriminating evidence. If evidence allows more than one element, say so.

> [!example]- Worked example
> An unknown Period 3 metal forms a white oxide. Its aqueous ions give a white precipitate with aqueous sodium hydroxide, which dissolves in excess alkali. Deduce the metal.
> A white precipitate alone is not decisive because both magnesium and aluminium ions can form one.
> Dissolution in excess alkali identifies amphoteric aluminium hydroxide. The metal is aluminium; its white oxide agrees with the other clue.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treat a white hydroxide precipitate as proof of aluminium. **Why it's wrong:** Magnesium hydroxide is also white. **Instead:** Check whether the precipitate dissolves in excess alkali.
- **Trap:** Reverse a group trend for an unfamiliar element. **Why it's wrong:** A prediction should follow the established trend unless evidence says otherwise. **Instead:** Write the direction down the group before comparing candidates.
- **Trap:** Identify an element from one clue and ignore the rest. **Why it's wrong:** Individual properties are often shared. **Instead:** Check all physical and chemical observations.

## Exam technique
For **predict**, give the likely property and the trend used. For **deduce**, show how the clues restrict period, group and identity. For **explain**, link a trend to nuclear attraction, distance or shielding, as appropriate. Distinguish an observation from an inference: a white precipitate is observed, while aluminium is a deduction that needs the excess-alkali result.
