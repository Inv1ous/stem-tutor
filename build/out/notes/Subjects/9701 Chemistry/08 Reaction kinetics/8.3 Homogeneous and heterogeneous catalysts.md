# 8.3 Homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysts
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction by offering a different mechanism with a lower activation energy. At a fixed temperature, more particles have enough energy to react, although their energy distribution has not changed.

## Key ideas
A **catalyst** is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction by providing an alternative reaction mechanism of lower activation energy, and is chemically unchanged at the end. **Catalysis** is the increase in rate caused by this alternative mechanism. A catalyst can participate in intermediate steps: being consumed in one step does not mean it is used up overall. Check whether it is regenerated later in the sequence.

Activation energy, $E_a$, is the minimum energy needed for a reaction to occur. On a reaction pathway diagram, measure the forward activation energy vertically from the reactant energy level to the relevant peak. For the reverse reaction, start at the product energy level. A catalyst can lower both forward and reverse activation energies. It does not alter the energies of the reactants or products, so the overall enthalpy change remains the same. It also does not change the equilibrium position.

![[Assets/9701/9701-8.3-pathway.svg]]

The **Boltzmann distribution** describes how particle energies are spread at a stated temperature. Adding a catalyst at that same temperature leaves the shape and peak of the distribution unchanged. Its lower $E_a$ threshold lies further left on the energy axis. The area under the curve to the right of that threshold is larger: a greater proportion of particles has enough energy to react. This explains a greater frequency of successful collisions and a faster reaction.

![[Assets/9701/9701-8.3-boltzmann.svg]]

In **homogeneous catalysis**, the catalyst and reactants are in the same phase. Concentrated sulfuric acid in a liquid esterification mixture is an example. In **heterogeneous catalysis**, they are in different phases. Solid vanadium(V) oxide catalysing the reaction of gaseous sulfur dioxide and oxygen in the Contact process is an example; reaction takes place at the catalyst surface. State the phases when classifying a catalyst, rather than relying on its name.

## Method
1. Mark the reactant and product energy levels on axes labelled energy and reaction pathway. Their difference is the enthalpy change, which stays fixed with a catalyst.
2. Draw the uncatalysed pathway with a higher peak and the catalysed pathway with a lower peak. A multi-step catalysed pathway may have more than one peak.
3. Measure each activation energy from the starting level of the direction requested to its relevant peak. In a multi-step pathway, the first step ends at the first peak.
4. On a Boltzmann diagram at fixed temperature, retain the same curve. Move only the activation-energy line left and compare areas to its right.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Reactants are at $40\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, products at $10\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$, and the first catalysed peak is at $75\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$. Calculate the forward activation energy.
> Start at the reactant level because the question asks for the forward reaction.
> $$E_a=75-40=35\ \mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}$$
> The reverse first-step barrier would instead be measured from the level at which that reverse step starts.

## Traps
- **Trap:** The catalyst shifts the Boltzmann curve. **Why it's wrong:** Temperature, not catalysis, determines that curve. **Instead:** Shift the $E_a$ threshold to the left.
- **Trap:** Measure decomposition activation energy from the synthesis reactants. **Why it's wrong:** Decomposition starts at the synthesis products. **Instead:** Identify the stated direction first.
- **Trap:** A catalyst changes reaction enthalpy. **Why it's wrong:** Both pathways share the same endpoints. **Instead:** Keep $Δ H$ identical.

## Exam technique
For **explain**, link the alternative mechanism, lower activation energy, larger fraction of particles above the threshold, and increased rate. For **sketch**, label both axes, endpoints, peaks, barriers and enthalpy change. For a catalyst cycle, follow all steps before deciding which species are regenerated. For homogeneous or heterogeneous catalysis, explicitly compare phases.
