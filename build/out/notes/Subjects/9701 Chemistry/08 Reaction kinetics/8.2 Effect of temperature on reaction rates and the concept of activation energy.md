# 8.2 Effect of temperature on reaction rates and the concept of activation energy
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Heating spreads molecular energies towards higher values. More collisions then meet the activation-energy threshold, so the frequency of effective collisions and the reaction rate increase.

## Key ideas
**Activation energy**, $E_A$, is the minimum energy required for a collision to be effective. A collision below this energy cannot produce reaction. It is a threshold for a particular reaction pathway, not the energy carried by every particle. At a fixed temperature, particles have different energies, so only some collisions meet the threshold.

The **Boltzmann distribution** describes the distribution of molecular energies in a sample. The horizontal axis is molecular energy and the vertical axis is the number or proportion of molecules with that energy. The curve starts at the origin, rises to a peak at the most probable energy, then tails away towards high energy. The area under the whole curve represents the total number of molecules (or a total proportion of one). For the same sample, that total area is constant. The area under the curve to the right of a vertical $E_A$ line represents particles with enough energy to react; it is an energy condition, not a guarantee that every such particle reacts on its next collision.

![[Assets/9701/9701-8.2-distribution.svg]]

When temperature increases, the distribution broadens. Its peak becomes lower and moves right, towards higher energy; the high-energy tail contains a greater proportion of molecules. The total area stays the same because no particles have been added. The $E_A$ line stays in the same place for an unchanged pathway. Hence the area to its right grows substantially.

## Method
To sketch and use a Boltzmann distribution:

1. Label molecular energy on the horizontal axis and number or proportion of molecules on the vertical axis. Start each curve at the origin and let its high-energy tail approach the axis.
2. For a higher temperature, draw a lower, broader peak farther to the right. Keep the total area the same. A lower-temperature curve is taller, narrower and peaks farther left.
3. Draw the same vertical $E_A$ line for both temperatures. Compare the areas under each curve to its right. The higher-temperature area is larger.
4. Translate the area comparison into collisions: a larger fraction of collisions has energy at least $E_A$, so effective collisions occur more frequently and the rate rises.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A gas reaction is heated without changing the reactants or catalyst. Sketch the new energy distribution and explain the faster rate.
> Draw molecular energy horizontally and number of molecules vertically. Keep the original curve for comparison. Add a curve with a lower peak farther right and a broader tail; preserve its total area. Keep the $E_A$ line fixed. Shade or identify the increased area to its right. More particles have energy at least $E_A$ and therefore the frequency of effective collisions increases. Faster-moving particles may also collide more frequently, but the increase in the fraction above $E_A$ is the main explanation.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Heating lowers activation energy. **Why it's wrong:** Temperature changes particle energies, not the threshold of the unchanged pathway. **Instead:** Keep $E_A$ fixed and change the distribution.
- **Trap:** A hotter sample has a taller peak. **Why it's wrong:** The energies are more spread out while the total number stays constant. **Instead:** Draw a lower, broader peak shifted right.
- **Trap:** Rate rises only because particles collide more often. **Why it's wrong:** This misses the much larger change in the fraction with sufficient energy. **Instead:** State that the frequency of effective collisions rises because more collisions exceed $E_A$.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, use the exact phrase “minimum energy required for a collision to be effective.” For **Sketch**, show the origin, axes, peak positions, high-energy tails, equal total areas and unchanged $E_A$. For **Explain**, connect temperature to the Boltzmann distribution, then to the proportion above $E_A$, then to effective-collision frequency and rate. Do not say that every particle gains the same amount of energy. If a catalyst is mentioned, distinguish its lower-energy pathway from a temperature change.
