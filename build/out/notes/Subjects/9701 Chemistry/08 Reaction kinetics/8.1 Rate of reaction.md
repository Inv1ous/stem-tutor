# 8.1 Rate of reaction
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Rate tells us how rapidly reactants disappear or products appear. Changes in concentration or gas pressure alter how often particles collide effectively, while measurements over time let us calculate the rate.

## Key ideas
**Rate of reaction** is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. It can also be expressed as change in an experimentally measured quantity, such as gas volume, per unit time. Its unit depends on the quantity measured: a concentration rate may be $\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$, while a gas-volume rate may be $\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}$. A positive rate of disappearance uses the negative of a reactant concentration graph's gradient because the concentration falls.

**Frequency of collisions** means the number of collisions per unit time. An **effective collision** leads to reaction and product formation: particles must collide with sufficient energy to overcome the activation energy and with a suitable orientation. A **non-effective collision** produces no reaction. A large number of collisions therefore does not mean every pair reacts. The rate depends on the frequency of *effective* collisions.

Increasing the concentration of a reactant in solution puts more of its particles into a given volume. Reactant particles meet more frequently, so there are more effective collisions per second and the rate usually increases. Dilution reverses this effect. Increasing the pressure of reacting gases by reducing their volume at constant temperature also puts more particles into each unit volume. Collisions become more frequent and so does reaction. Constant temperature matters: increased pressure by compression does not itself give particles more kinetic energy. For a gaseous mixture, adding an inert gas at constant volume and temperature leaves the reacting gases' concentrations unchanged, so it does not increase their mutual collision frequency.

## Method
1. Identify the quantity measured and the start and end times. Subtract the starting value from the ending value. For disappearance of a reactant, take the magnitude of its decrease.
2. Divide the change by the elapsed time: $\text{average rate}=\Delta\text{quantity}/\Delta t$. Convert minutes to seconds if the requested unit uses seconds. State the unit that follows from the measured quantity.
3. On a curved graph, an average rate is the gradient of a chord joining two times. For an **instantaneous rate**, draw a tangent at the specified time and calculate its gradient using two well-separated points on that tangent.
4. When the measured substance differs from the substance named in the question, use the balanced equation. For $\ce{2H2O2(aq) -> 2H2O(l) + O2(g)}$, twice as many moles of peroxide disappear as moles of oxygen appear. At r.t.p., convert gas volume using $V_m=24.0\ \mathrm{dm^3\,mol^{-1}}$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A product concentration rises from $0.030$ to $0.150\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$ in $40\ \mathrm{s}$. Calculate its average formation rate.
> Change in concentration $=0.150-0.030=0.120\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}$.
> Rate $=0.120/40=0.0030\ \mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}$.
> This is an average over the whole interval, not the rate at a single instant.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Every collision produces products. **Why it's wrong:** Some collisions lack enough energy or suitable orientation. **Instead:** Link the rate to the frequency of effective collisions.
- **Trap:** More concentrated particles move faster. **Why it's wrong:** At constant temperature their energy distribution is unchanged. **Instead:** Explain the greater number of collisions per unit time.
- **Trap:** Use the oxygen production rate as the peroxide disappearance rate. **Why it's wrong:** The balanced equation has a $2:1$ mole ratio. **Instead:** Apply the coefficient before dividing by time.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, give a precise rate definition including “per unit time”. For **Explain**, connect the changed particle density to collision frequency, then to effective collisions and rate. For **Calculate**, show subtraction, time conversion, any mole ratio and the final unit. A product graph slopes upward; a reactant graph slopes downward, but report its disappearance rate as positive.
