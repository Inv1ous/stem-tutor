# 4.2 Bonding and structure
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A solid's particles and the attractions between them determine its properties. Use several observed properties together to deduce its structure.

## Key ideas
A **giant ionic lattice** is a regular three-dimensional array of oppositely charged ions. Sodium chloride contains $\ce{Na+}$ and $\ce{Cl-}$; magnesium oxide contains $\ce{Mg^2+}$ and $\ce{O^2-}$. Strong electrostatic attraction acts throughout each lattice. Neither is made of separate molecules.

A **simple molecular lattice** has separate molecules in regular positions, held together by intermolecular forces. Solid iodine contains $\ce{I2}$ molecules held by dispersion forces. Buckminsterfullerene consists of separate $\ce{C60}$ cages: strong covalent bonds are *within* each cage, but much weaker forces act *between* cages. Ice contains separate $\ce{H2O}$ molecules held in an open lattice by hydrogen bonds. The existence of covalent bonds inside a molecule does not make its crystal giant covalent.

A **giant molecular**, or giant covalent, lattice is one continuous covalent network. In $\ce{SiO2}$ each silicon bonds to four oxygen atoms, and each oxygen bridges two silicon atoms. Diamond has a tetrahedral network with four bonds per carbon and no mobile electrons. Graphite has layers: each carbon bonds to three others, leaving an electron delocalised across a layer; weak forces separate the layers. A **giant metallic lattice**, such as copper, has positive metal ions attracted to mobile delocalised electrons.

## Method
1. Identify the particles and the force overcome on melting or boiling. Molecular solids typically melt at lower temperatures because only intermolecular forces are overcome. Giant ionic, metallic and covalent structures require much more energy to disrupt. Ice's hydrogen bonds make its melting point relatively high for a simple molecular solid.
2. Check conductivity in several states. Metals conduct as solids and liquids through delocalised electrons. Ionic solids do not conduct because ions are fixed; their melts and aqueous solutions conduct through mobile ions. Diamond and $\ce{SiO2}$ lack mobile charge carriers. Graphite conducts along its layers.
3. Check solubility. Many ionic solids dissolve in water when hydration stabilises separated ions, but not all do: $\ce{MgO}$ is only sparingly soluble. Molecular solubility depends on solvent interactions; iodine is poorly soluble in water. Giant covalent networks are generally insoluble. Use solubility with the other tests, never alone.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A brittle solid has a high melting point, does not conduct as a solid, conducts when molten and dissolves in water. Deduce its structure.
> The high melting point suggests a giant lattice with strong attractions. Conduction only after melting means charged particles are fixed in the solid but mobile in the liquid. These are ions, so the solid has a giant ionic lattice. Water solubility supports this deduction.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Melting iodine breaks its I–I bonds. **Why it's wrong:** Melting separates intact $\ce{I2}$ molecules. **Instead:** Name the intermolecular forces overcome.
- **Trap:** A high melting point proves a substance conducts. **Why it's wrong:** Conduction requires mobile charge carriers. **Instead:** Ask whether ions or electrons can move in the tested state.
- **Trap:** Every covalent solid is giant covalent. **Why it's wrong:** $\ce{C60}$ is a crystal of separate covalent molecules. **Instead:** Decide whether covalent bonds connect the whole lattice or only each molecule.

## Exam technique
For **Describe**, state both the particles and the attractions. For **Explain**, connect a structural feature to the stated property: say *mobile ions* or *delocalised electrons*, not just “has charges”. For **Deduce**, cite at least two observations and explain how they support the same structure. Compare solid and molten conductivity before using melting point or solubility as supporting evidence.
