# 4.1 The gaseous state: ideal and real gases and pV = nRT
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Moving gas molecules strike container walls and so exert pressure. The ideal gas equation connects that pressure with volume, amount and absolute temperature.

## Key ideas
A gas molecule moves continually. When it strikes a container wall and rebounds, its momentum changes. The wall experiences a force. **Gas pressure** is this average force per unit area of wall. Collisions among molecules can change their paths, but pressure *on the container* comes from collisions with its walls. Faster or more frequent wall collisions increase the average force and therefore the pressure.

An **ideal gas** is a model whose particles have **zero particle volume** and **no intermolecular forces of attraction**. Zero volume does not mean zero mass: particles have mass, move, and transfer momentum in collisions. Real molecules do occupy space and attract one another. At low pressure they are far apart, so their own volume matters less than the container volume. At high temperature their kinetic energy makes attractive forces relatively less important. Consequently, real gases tend to behave more ideally at high temperature and low pressure. Steam has stronger effective hydrogen bonding than ammonia in a past-paper comparison and deviates more under the stated conditions.

For an ideal gas,
$$
pV=nRT
$$
Here $p$ is pressure in pascals, $V$ is volume in cubic metres, $n$ is amount in moles, and $T$ is temperature in kelvin. Use $R=8.31\,\mathrm{J\,K^{-1}\,mol^{-1}}$. Since $1\,\mathrm{Pa\,m^3}=1\,\mathrm{J}$, these units fit the equation. The kelvin temperature is Celsius temperature plus 273 for these examination calculations. Also, $1\,\mathrm{dm^3}=10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ and $1\,\mathrm{cm^3}=10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.

## Method
1. Write $pV=nRT$ and rearrange for the quantity requested, such as $n=pV/(RT)$ or $V=nRT/p$.
2. Convert pressure to Pa, volume to m³ and temperature to K before substituting. Convert a final volume back to dm³ or cm³ if requested.
3. If a gas of mass $m$ grams has unknown relative molecular mass, use $n=m/M_r$. Combining this with $pV=nRT$ gives $M_r=mRT/(pV)$. Keep $m$ in grams, so the numerical result is the relative molecular mass.
4. For a gas formed by a reaction, find the limiting reagent and apply the balanced equation. The amount of gaseous product need not equal the amount of starting material.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A vapour has mass $0.175\,\mathrm{g}$, volume $4.50\times10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^3}$, temperature $373\,\mathrm{K}$ and pressure $101300\,\mathrm{Pa}$. Determine its $M_r$.
> $$
> M_r=\frac{mRT}{pV}=\frac{0.175\times8.31\times373}{101300\times4.50\times10^{-5}}=119
> $$
> The volume is already in m³. $M_r$ has no unit and is reported to three significant figures.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating zero particle volume as zero mass. **Why it's wrong:** Mass and momentum are needed for wall collisions. **Instead:** Say that ideal particles have zero *own volume* and no attractions.
- **Trap:** Inserting $240\,\mathrm{cm^3}$ as 240 in the SI equation. **Why it's wrong:** $pV$ would then have the wrong units. **Instead:** Convert it to $2.40\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}$.
- **Trap:** Using the gas amount as the reagent amount. **Why it's wrong:** A balanced equation may have a different mole ratio. **Instead:** Calculate gas moles, then apply the coefficients.

## Exam technique
For **Explain** questions about pressure, link wall collisions to momentum change, force and force per unit area. For **State** questions about ideal gases, give both particle assumptions explicitly. For **Calculate** questions, show the rearranged equation, conversions, substitution and final unit or unitless $M_r$; this makes a factor-of-1000 error easy to spot.
