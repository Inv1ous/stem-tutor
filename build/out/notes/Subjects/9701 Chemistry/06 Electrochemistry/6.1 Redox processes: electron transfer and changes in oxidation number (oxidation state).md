# 6.1 Redox processes
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Oxidation means electron loss and an increase in oxidation number; reduction means electron gain and a decrease. The electron changes are equal and opposite in a redox reaction, which lets you identify agents and balance equations.

## Key ideas

An **oxidation number** is an assigned number that tracks the electrons attributed to an atom in a compound or ion. An uncombined element has oxidation number $0$. A monatomic ion has its ionic charge. In most compounds, hydrogen is $+1$ and oxygen is $-2$; exceptions include metal hydrides (H is $-1$) and peroxides (O is $-1$). The sum of oxidation numbers, multiplied by the number of each atom, equals the charge on the whole species. For $\ce{SO4^{2-}}$, sulfur satisfies $x+4(-2)=-2$, so $x=+6$.

**Oxidation** is loss of electrons: oxidation number rises. **Reduction** is gain of electrons: oxidation number falls. Both happen together in a **redox** reaction. In $\ce{Zn + Cu^{2+} -> Zn^{2+} + Cu}$, zinc changes from $0$ to $+2$ and donates two electrons; copper changes from $+2$ to $0$ and accepts them. Zinc is the **reducing agent** because it reduces copper(II) and is itself oxidised. Copper(II) is the **oxidising agent** because it oxidises zinc and is itself reduced.

In **disproportionation**, the same element in one reactant is both oxidised and reduced. For $\ce{Cl2 + H2O <=> HOCl + H+ + Cl-}$, chlorine starts at $0$ and ends at $+1$ in $\ce{HOCl}$ and $-1$ in $\ce{Cl-}$. A reaction name such as addition or hydrolysis does not by itself decide whether redox occurs: inspect oxidation numbers.

A Roman numeral after an element name gives the **magnitude** of its oxidation number. In iron(III) chloride, $\ce{FeCl3}$, three chlorides each contribute $-1$, so iron is $+3$ and the numeral is III. In $\ce{Cu2O}$, oxygen is $-2$ and each copper is $+1$: copper(I) oxide.

## Method

1. Assign known oxidation numbers and set their sum equal to the species charge. Keep negative signs when finding a change: nitrogen goes from $-3$ in $\ce{NH4+}$ to $+5$ in $\ce{NO3-}$, a rise of $+8$.
2. For an equation, find the element oxidised and the element reduced. Multiply each change by its atom count, then choose coefficients so total electron loss equals total electron gain.
3. Balance remaining atoms using water and, in acidic solution, $\ce{H+}$ where appropriate. Finally check **both** atom counts and total charge. In alkaline solution, use $\ce{OH-}$ as appropriate.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Balance $\ce{ClO3- + MnO4- + H+ -> ClO4- + MnO2 + H2O}$.
> Chlorine rises from $+5$ to $+7$: two electrons lost per ion. Manganese falls from $+7$ to $+4$: three electrons gained per ion.
> The least common multiple is six, so use three chlorate ions and two manganate(VII) ions. Balance O and H with one water and two hydrogen ions:
> $$
> \ce{3ClO3- + 2MnO4- + 2H+ -> 3ClO4- + 2MnO2 + H2O}
> $$
> Left and right each have charge $-3$, and each element's atoms balance.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Subtract magnitudes to find a change from $-3$ to $+5$. **Why it's wrong:** the initial value is negative. **Instead:** calculate $+5-(-3)=+8$.
- **Trap:** Call the oxidising agent the electron donor. **Why it's wrong:** the oxidising agent gains electrons and is reduced. **Instead:** follow the electrons to the acceptor.
- **Trap:** Stop when the atom counts match. **Why it's wrong:** ionic charge can still be unequal. **Instead:** check charge after balancing H and O.
- **Trap:** Call any reaction with oxidation and reduction disproportionation. **Why it's wrong:** disproportionation requires the **same element in one reactant** to change in both directions.

## Exam technique

For **calculate**, show the oxidation-number sum before solving. For **explain**, give the oxidation-number change or electron direction for each species, then name the process or agent. For **balance**, state the electron changes and show the coefficient ratio before adding water and hydrogen ions; a final charge check catches a common mark-losing error.
