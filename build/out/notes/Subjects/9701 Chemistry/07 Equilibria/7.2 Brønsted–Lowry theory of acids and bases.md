# 7.2 Brønsted–Lowry theory of acids and bases
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Acids donate protons and bases accept them. The extent of dissociation determines strength, while the shape of a titration curve determines the indicator.

## Key ideas
The four common acids are hydrochloric acid, $\ce{HCl}$; sulfuric acid, $\ce{H2SO4}$; nitric acid, $\ce{HNO3}$; and ethanoic acid, $\ce{CH3COOH}$. Common alkalis are sodium hydroxide, $\ce{NaOH}$; potassium hydroxide, $\ce{KOH}$; and aqueous ammonia, $\ce{NH3}$. Memorise each name with its formula. An alkali is a base that produces hydroxide ions in aqueous solution.

In Brønsted–Lowry theory, an acid is a proton donor and a base is a proton acceptor. In $\ce{NH3 (aq) + H2O (l) <=> NH4+ (aq) + OH- (aq)}$, ammonia accepts a proton while water donates one. A species can act differently in a different reaction; identify the actual proton transfer.

Strong acids and strong bases are fully dissociated in aqueous solution. Weak acids and weak bases are partially dissociated. Hydrochloric and nitric acids are strong; ethanoic acid is weak. Sodium and potassium hydroxide are strong bases, while ammonia is a weak base. Strength describes the fraction dissociated, whereas concentration describes the amount dissolved per unit volume.

At 298 K, water has pH 7; acid solutions have pH below 7 and alkaline solutions have pH above 7. At equal analytical concentration, a strong acid usually gives a lower pH than a weak acid because it provides more $\ce{H+ (aq)}$. With the same reactive metal, it normally gives faster initial hydrogen production. A pH meter or universal indicator distinguishes pH; conductivity also tends to be greater because the strong acid produces more ions. Control concentration, volume and temperature for a fair comparison.

Neutralisation is $\ce{H+ (aq) + OH- (aq) -> H2O (l)}$. The remaining ions form a salt. For example, $\ce{HCl (aq) + NaOH (aq) -> NaCl (aq) + H2O (l)}$ forms sodium chloride. The name of the salt follows the base cation and acid anion.

## Method
1. To sketch a pH titration curve, label pH vertically and volume of titrant horizontally. Decide whether acid or alkali is initially in the flask; adding alkali makes pH rise.
2. Mark starting pH, a buffer region if a weak reagent is present, the steep region and the final pH. Strong acid–strong alkali gives a steep rise centred at pH 7. Weak acid–strong alkali starts higher and has an equivalence point above pH 7. Strong acid–weak alkali has equivalence below pH 7. Weak acid–weak alkali has a shallow change with no sharp jump. With acid added to alkali, the curve falls instead.
3. To select an indicator from given colour-change ranges, choose one whose transition lies wholly within the steep pH change around equivalence. Phenolphthalein often suits weak acid–strong alkali; methyl orange often suits strong acid–weak alkali. Several indicators suit strong–strong. A weak–weak titration has no reliable visual indicator end point. No $pK_a$ calculation is needed.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Ethanoic acid in the flask is titrated with sodium hydroxide. Sketch a curve beginning at a moderately acidic pH, rising gradually through a buffer region, then rising sharply around equivalence above pH 7. Finish in the alkaline range. Phenolphthalein changes colour inside the steep rise, so it is suitable.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling a dilute strong acid weak. **Why it's wrong:** Dilution changes concentration, not the fraction dissociated. **Instead:** State whether dissociation is full or partial.
- **Trap:** Putting every equivalence point at pH 7. **Why it's wrong:** The salt from a weak acid or base can affect pH. **Instead:** Identify the strong and weak partners.
- **Trap:** Choosing an indicator simply because its range contains pH 7. **Why it's wrong:** The equivalence pH may differ. **Instead:** Match its full transition range to the steep section.

## Exam technique
For *describe*, state the observable curve features and axes. For *explain*, connect dissociation to ion concentration and the observation. For *sketch*, show the correct initial and final pH and equivalence region. For *select*, cite the indicator's transition range relative to the steep section.
