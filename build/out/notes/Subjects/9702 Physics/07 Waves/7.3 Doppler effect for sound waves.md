# 7.3 Doppler effect for sound waves
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A moving source of sound changes the spacing of successive wavefronts reaching a stationary observer. The observed frequency is higher during approach and lower during recession, even though the source emits at a constant frequency.

## Key ideas
The **Doppler effect** is the change in observed frequency when a source of waves moves relative to an observer. Here the observer is stationary and the sound source moves. The **source frequency**, $f_s$, is the rate at which the source emits waves. The **observed frequency**, $f_o$, is the rate at which wavefronts reach the listener. These need not be equal.

As the source travels towards the listener, each new wavefront starts closer to the listener than the previous one did. The wavefronts in front are closer together: their wavelength is shorter. Since $v=f\lambda$ and the speed $v$ of sound in the medium stays fixed, the observed frequency rises. Behind a receding source, successive wavefronts are spaced farther apart, so the observed frequency falls. Source motion does not change the speed of sound in the medium.

For motion directly towards or away from a stationary observer, use

$$f_o=\frac{f_s v}{v\pm v_s}$$

Here $v_s$ is the *speed of the source* and $v$ is the *speed of sound*. Use $v-v_s$ for approach and $v+v_s$ for recession. The smaller approach denominator makes $f_o>f_s$; the larger recession denominator makes $f_o<f_s$. These comparisons are a useful check on every numerical result. This syllabus requires a moving source and stationary observer; the expression here is for that arrangement.

## Method
1. Identify whether the source is moving towards or away from the stationary observer at the instant considered.
2. Write the matching formula before inserting numbers: minus for approach, plus for recession.
3. Put the emitted frequency in $f_s$, the speed of sound in $v$, and the source speed in $v_s$; keep both speeds in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
4. Calculate $f_o$, include $\mathrm{Hz}$, and compare it with $f_s$ to check the direction of the shift.
5. If a source passes the observer and the question asks for the *difference*, calculate an approach frequency and a recession frequency, then subtract the latter from the former.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A horn emits at $600\,\mathrm{Hz}$ and approaches a stationary listener at $20.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. The speed of sound is $340\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
> For approach, subtract the source speed in the denominator.
> $$f_o=\frac{600(340)}{340-20.0}=637.5\,\mathrm{Hz}$$
> This is greater than $600\,\mathrm{Hz}$, as expected. If the horn instead receded at the same speed, the denominator would be $340+20.0$ and the heard frequency would be lower.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Using the plus sign for an approaching source. **Why it's wrong:** Approach compresses wavefront spacing. **Instead:** Use $v-v_s$ and check that the answer exceeds $f_s$.
- **Trap:** Saying that a moving source makes sound itself travel faster. **Why it's wrong:** The propagation speed in the same medium remains fixed. **Instead:** Describe the change in wavelength and arrival rate.
- **Trap:** Subtracting the source frequency from the approach frequency for a passing horn. **Why it's wrong:** The minimum heard frequency is the recession frequency. **Instead:** Calculate both observed frequencies and take their difference.
- **Trap:** Assuming slowing approach raises the pitch because the source is still coming closer. **Why it's wrong:** The approach shift becomes smaller as source speed falls. **Instead:** Track the changing denominator. Faster recession lowers the pitch further.

## Exam technique
For **Explain**, connect source motion to wavefront spacing, then to frequency using unchanged sound speed. For **Calculate**, show the selected equation and substitution, give a frequency in $\mathrm{Hz}$, and check whether it lies above or below the emitted frequency. When motion is at an angle, only the component of source velocity directed towards or away from the observer produces the instantaneous shift.
