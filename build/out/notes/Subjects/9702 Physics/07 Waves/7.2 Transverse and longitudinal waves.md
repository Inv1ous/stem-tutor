# 7.2 Transverse and longitudinal waves
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Classify a wave by comparing the direction in which its particles oscillate with the direction in which its energy travels. A graph of displacement can describe either type; read its axes before imagining physical motion.

## Key ideas
A **transverse wave** has particle oscillations **perpendicular** to the direction of energy transfer. A wave travelling along a stretched rope can make the rope elements move up and down while the disturbance moves horizontally. An electromagnetic wave is transverse and can travel through a vacuum. A **longitudinal wave** has particle oscillations **parallel** to the direction of energy transfer. In sound travelling through air, individual air particles move back and forth along the line of propagation. Sound requires a medium. Both kinds transfer energy without a net transfer of the medium, and both may show wave effects such as diffraction. Transverse waves can be polarised by selecting one direction of transverse vibration; a longitudinal wave has no such vibration direction to select.

In a longitudinal wave, a **compression** is a region in which particles are closer together than at equilibrium; a **rarefaction** is a region in which they are further apart. Consecutive compression centres are one wavelength $\lambda$ apart, as are consecutive rarefaction centres. A compression centre and its nearest rarefaction centre are $\lambda/2$ apart. These distances are measured *along* the direction of propagation.

## Method
1. Read the horizontal and vertical axes. A displacement–distance graph is a snapshot of many particles at one instant. A displacement–time graph follows one particle as the wave passes.
2. Establish the physical direction of vibration from the question. The vertical height of a plotted longitudinal displacement graph represents signed displacement *along* the direction of travel; it does not turn sound into a transverse wave.
3. For a right-moving profile, imagine shifting the entire snapshot a small distance right while keeping the particle's position fixed. At a positive displacement–distance slope the particle's displacement initially decreases; at a negative slope it initially increases. At a crest or trough it is momentarily stationary.
4. To locate density changes on a longitudinal displacement graph, consider neighbouring particles: where their displacements bring them closer together, there is a compression. This occurs at a negative spatial slope when positive displacement means motion to the right. A positive slope indicates a rarefaction.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Adjacent compression centres in a sound wave are $0.80\,\mathrm{m}$ apart. Determine the distance from a compression centre to its nearest rarefaction centre.
> The compression spacing is one wavelength, so $\lambda=0.80\,\mathrm{m}$.
> The nearest rarefaction is half a cycle away:
> $$
> d=\frac{\lambda}{2}=\frac{0.80}{2}=0.40\,\mathrm{m}.
> $$

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling a longitudinal displacement graph transverse because its trace rises and falls. **Why it's wrong:** Graph height encodes a value, not the physical direction of vibration. **Instead:** Use the stated direction of particle displacement relative to energy transfer.
- **Trap:** Assuming every particle moves with the travelling wave. **Why it's wrong:** The profile travels, but each particle oscillates about equilibrium. **Instead:** Shift the profile slightly in its travel direction to infer a fixed particle's next displacement.
- **Trap:** Reading a compression as a displacement crest. **Why it's wrong:** Compression describes particle spacing. **Instead:** Look for converging neighbouring displacements, or a negative displacement–distance slope with right positive.

## Exam technique
For **Compare**, give both directions explicitly: perpendicular for transverse and parallel for longitudinal, each relative to energy transfer. For **Determine** from a graph, state whether the horizontal axis is distance or time, identify the particle's initial displacement, then infer its next motion. At a maximum displacement, the instantaneous particle speed is zero; at zero displacement in a sinusoidal wave, its speed is greatest. When measuring wavelength on a particle-spacing diagram, use matching successive features rather than a compression and its adjacent rarefaction.
