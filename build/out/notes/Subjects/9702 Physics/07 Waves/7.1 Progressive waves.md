# 7.1 Progressive waves
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A progressive wave is a travelling disturbance that transfers energy. Particles of a medium vibrate about equilibrium while the wave moves on.

## Key ideas
Wave motion is visible when a pulse travels along a rope, a disturbance travels through a spring, or ripples spread across a tank. In each case, the disturbance progresses; an individual particle of the rope, spring or water vibrates about its equilibrium position. A progressive wave transfers energy from place to place. The medium itself does not travel with the wave.

**Displacement** is a particle's distance and direction from equilibrium at an instant. **Amplitude** $a$ is its maximum displacement from equilibrium; peak-to-peak displacement is $2a$. **Period** $T$ is the time for one complete oscillation. **Frequency** $f$ is the number of complete oscillations per unit time, so $f=1/T$. **Wavelength** $\lambda$ is the distance between consecutive points in phase. **Phase difference** describes the angular separation of two oscillations: one full cycle is $360^\circ$ or $2\pi$ rad. A separation of $\lambda/4$ corresponds to $90^\circ$. **Wave speed** is the distance a wavefront travels per unit time.

One wavelength passes a point in one period. Therefore $v=\lambda/T=f\lambda$. This derives the wave equation from the definitions of speed, period and frequency. Use $v=f\lambda$ with $v$ in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, $f$ in Hz and $\lambda$ in m. At a fixed wave speed, increasing frequency decreases wavelength.

**Intensity** $I$ is power transferred per unit area normal to the direction of travel: $I=P/A$, in $\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}$. For comparable progressive waves in the same medium at the same frequency, $I\propto a^2$. Doubling amplitude therefore multiplies intensity by four; halving intensity multiplies amplitude by $1/\sqrt{2}$.

## Method
1. On a cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO), read the **time-base** as time per horizontal division. Count divisions between matching points of successive cycles, multiply to get $T$, and calculate $f=1/T$.
2. Read **y-gain** as voltage per vertical division. Count from the centre line to a peak and multiply to obtain voltage amplitude. A microphone calibration is needed to convert this voltage into sound-particle displacement amplitude.
3. For wave calculations, convert all times to seconds and lengths to metres before substituting in $v=f\lambda$ or $I=P/A$. State a suitable unit and significant figures.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A CRO shows one cycle across 4.0 divisions with time-base $0.50\,\mathrm{ms}$ per division. Determine frequency.
> $T=4.0\times0.50=2.0\,\mathrm{ms}=2.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}$.
> $f=1/T=1/(2.0\times10^{-3})=500\,\mathrm{Hz}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Reading peak-to-peak height as amplitude. **Why it's wrong:** Amplitude is measured from equilibrium to a peak. **Instead:** Halve the peak-to-peak height.
- **Trap:** Treating a CRO voltage trace as a direct measure of sound displacement. **Why it's wrong:** Y-gain gives volts per division. **Instead:** Use microphone calibration if displacement in metres is required.
- **Trap:** Halving amplitude when intensity halves. **Why it's wrong:** Intensity depends on amplitude squared. **Instead:** Take the square root of the intensity ratio.
- **Trap:** Measuring half a cycle as the period. **Why it's wrong:** A period is one complete oscillation. **Instead:** Measure between successive peaks or equivalent crossings.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, state the precise quantity: wavelength requires consecutive points in phase, and period requires a complete oscillation. For **Show (that)**, write $v=\lambda/T$ and $f=1/T$ before reaching $v=f\lambda$. For **Calculate**, show unit conversions, particularly milliseconds to seconds and centimetres to metres. A displacement–time graph shows period along its horizontal axis; a displacement–distance graph shows wavelength. Read the axis label before choosing a quantity.
