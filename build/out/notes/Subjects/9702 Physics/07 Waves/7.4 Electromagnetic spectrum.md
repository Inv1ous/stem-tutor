# 7.4 Electromagnetic spectrum
> [!abstract] In one breath
> All electromagnetic waves are transverse and travel at the same speed in free space. Their regions differ in wavelength and frequency, and only a narrow interval is visible to the human eye.

## Key ideas
An **electromagnetic wave** is transverse: its oscillations are perpendicular to its direction of travel. All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in free space, $c=3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Radio waves and gamma rays share this speed even though their wavelengths differ enormously. A wave's frequency and wavelength obey $c=f\lambda$ in free space. If wavelength increases, frequency decreases; a longer wavelength does not mean a lower free-space speed.

In order of **increasing wavelength**, the principal regions are gamma rays, X-rays, ultraviolet, visible light, infrared, microwaves, then radio waves. Reverse this list for increasing frequency. These are broad, approximate bands, and the boundaries are not perfectly sharp. Useful free-space scales are gamma rays below about $10^{-11}$ m; X-rays around $10^{-11}$ to $10^{-8}$ m; ultraviolet around $10^{-8}$ to $4\times10^{-7}$ m; visible light $4\times10^{-7}$ to $7\times10^{-7}$ m; infrared from about $7\times10^{-7}$ m to 1 mm; microwaves about 1 mm to 30 cm; and radio waves about 30 cm to 100 km. Remember both the order and a few anchors rather than trying to treat every boundary as exact.

**Visible light** has wavelengths of approximately **400–700 nm in free space**. Since $1\ \mathrm{nm}=10^{-9}\ \mathrm m$, this is $0.4$–$0.7\ \mathrm{\mu m}$. Shorter wavelengths lie beyond the violet end in ultraviolet; longer wavelengths lie beyond the red end in infrared. The eye does not detect the rest of the electromagnetic spectrum directly.

## Method
1. Convert the given length to metres or nanometres, or convert a frequency using $\lambda=c/f$.
2. Locate the wavelength against the visible interval and the approximate principal-region ranges.
3. When comparing two waves in free space, keep $c$ fixed. A change in wavelength implies an inverse change in frequency.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A wave has frequency $5.0\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$ in free space. Identify its region.
> Use $\lambda=c/f$ because frequency is given and the regions are organised by wavelength.
> $$\lambda=\frac{3.00\times10^8}{5.0\times10^{14}}=6.0\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm m=600\ \mathrm{nm}$$
> The wavelength is between 400 and 700 nm, so the wave is visible light.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling 500 nm ultraviolet because the number looks small. **Why it's wrong:** 500 nm lies inside 400–700 nm. **Instead:** convert first and compare with both visible limits.
- **Trap:** Calling a 2 cm wave radio. **Why it's wrong:** 2 cm falls within the approximate 1 mm–30 cm microwave band. **Instead:** use the principal-region scale.
- **Trap:** Giving different vacuum speeds to different colours or regions. **Why it's wrong:** every electromagnetic wave travels at $c$ in free space. **Instead:** use $c=f\lambda$ to distinguish frequency from speed.

## Exam technique
For **State** or **Give**, name the property or region directly. For **Calculate**, write $c=f\lambda$, substitute $3.00\times10^8\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, show the conversion and give a sensible number of significant figures. For **Explain**, say explicitly that speed is the same in free space and show how frequency and wavelength compensate. Check prefixes carefully: GHz is $10^9$ Hz, THz is $10^{12}$ Hz, and nm is $10^{-9}$ m.
