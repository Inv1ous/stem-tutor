# 11.1 Atoms, nuclei and radiation
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Scattering reveals a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus within an otherwise mostly empty atom. Nuclear equations track nucleons and charge while emitted particles carry away energy.

## Key ideas
Most α-particles fired at thin metal foil pass straight through: atoms are mostly empty space. A very small fraction turn through large angles or rebound, showing that positive charge and most atomic mass occupy a tiny nucleus. These observations do not reveal the separate existence of neutrons.

The nuclear atom has protons and neutrons in its nucleus and orbital electrons outside it. A neutral atom has as many electrons as protons. **Proton number** $Z$ is the number of protons; **nucleon number** $A$ is the total number of protons and neutrons. Hence the neutron number is $N=A-Z$. In ${}^{A}_{Z}X$, $X$ names the element, while the upper and lower numbers give $A$ and $Z$. Isotopes are forms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers, so their nucleon numbers differ.

An α-particle is a helium nucleus with two protons and two neutrons. It has mass about $4u$ and charge $+2e$. A β− particle is an electron of mass $m_e$ and charge $-e$; a β+ particle is a positron with the same mass and charge $+e$. γ-radiation consists of electromagnetic photons, which have zero rest mass and zero charge. An antiparticle has the same mass as its corresponding particle and opposite charge; the positron is the electron's antiparticle.

In β− decay, a neutron becomes a proton and an electron and electron antineutrino are emitted. In β+ decay, a proton becomes a neutron and a positron and electron neutrino are emitted. The β particle takes a variable share of the available energy because an (anti)neutrino also carries energy: β energies form a continuous spectrum. α particles from a given transition have discrete energies. The unified atomic mass unit $u$ is one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom; $1u=1.66\times10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

## Method
In all nuclear processes, **conservation of nucleon number** and **conservation of charge** mean that the totals on the two sides of the equation are equal.

1. Read $A$ and $Z$ from the parent nuclide and identify every emitted particle.
2. Balance total nucleon number and total electric charge on both sides. For α emission subtract four from $A$ and two from $Z$. For β− emission keep $A$ and increase daughter $Z$ by one; for β+ keep $A$ and decrease daughter $Z$ by one.
3. Include the electron antineutrino with β− or electron neutrino with β+. Check every symbol and both sums.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Uranium-238 emits an α-particle. The daughter nucleon number is $238-4=234$, and its proton number is $92-2=90$, identifying thorium.
> $$
> {}^{238}_{92}U\rightarrow{}^{234}_{90}Th+{}^{4}_{2}\alpha
> $$
> Both sides have nucleon number 238 and charge number 92.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Large-angle scattering means atoms are solid. **Why it's wrong:** Almost all α-particles cross the foil. **Instead:** Infer a very small, concentrated nucleus.
- **Trap:** $A$ counts neutrons. **Why it's wrong:** It counts all nucleons. **Instead:** Use $N=A-Z$.
- **Trap:** β+ emission raises daughter $Z$ or emits an antineutrino. **Why it's wrong:** A proton becomes a neutron and emits a positron and neutrino. **Instead:** Lower daughter $Z$ by one and write $\nu_e$.

## Exam technique
When asked to **describe** the atom, locate protons, neutrons and orbital electrons explicitly. To **explain** scattering, connect each observation to its inference. To **calculate** a daughter nuclide, write separate $A$ and charge balances, including any incoming particle. State the type of neutrino for each β process.
