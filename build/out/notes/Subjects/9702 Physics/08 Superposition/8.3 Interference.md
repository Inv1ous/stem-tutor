# 8.3 Interference
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Waves that overlap add their displacements. Coherent sources maintain a fixed phase relationship, so their constructive and destructive interference forms a stable pattern.

## Key ideas
**Interference** is the superposition of two or more waves to produce a resultant wave with regions of reinforcement and cancellation. At a maximum, waves arrive in phase; at a minimum, they arrive in antiphase. **Coherent** waves have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. That phase difference need not be zero, and their amplitudes need not be equal. Equal amplitudes are needed for complete cancellation at a minimum.

For two sources initially in phase, a path difference of $n\lambda$ gives a maximum, while $(n+\tfrac12)\lambda$ gives a minimum, where $n$ is an integer. An initial phase difference shifts these positions. A visible, stationary fringe pattern needs overlapping waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference. Similar amplitudes make its bright and dark regions easier to distinguish. For light, use a monochromatic source and a common source feeding both slits; a broad mixture of wavelengths can blur fringes.

## Method
1. **Water:** Attach two dippers in a ripple tank to one vibrator or motor. Illuminate the surface and use a stroboscope or suitable camera to reveal fixed nodal and antinodal lines.
2. **Sound:** Connect two loudspeakers to one signal generator. Move a microphone, preferably linked to an oscilloscope, across the overlap region. Alternating high and low amplitudes reveal interference.
3. **Light:** Illuminate two narrow slits from the same monochromatic source. Their diffracted light overlaps on a distant screen, giving alternating bright and dark fringes.
4. **Microwaves:** Drive two sources from the same oscillator and move a receiver through the overlap region. It detects alternating maxima and minima. In every setup, a shared drive maintains the relative phase.

For double-slit light, measure the slit separation $a$, screen distance $D$, and spacing $x$ between adjacent bright fringes (or adjacent dark fringes). Then use $\lambda=ax/D$, with all lengths in metres. To reduce uncertainty, measure across several intervals and divide by the number of intervals. Since $x=\lambda D/a$, increasing $D$ or wavelength widens fringes, while increasing $a$ narrows them.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Slits $0.20\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart produce fringes $6.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart on a screen $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ away. Calculate the wavelength.
> Use $\lambda=ax/D$. Convert $a=0.20\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$ and $x=6.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$.
> $$\lambda=\frac{(0.20\times10^{-3})(6.0\times10^{-3})}{2.0}=6.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}.$$
> The answer is $600\,\mathrm{nm}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Coherent means exactly in phase. **Why it's wrong:** Coherence permits any *constant* phase difference. **Instead:** State both equal frequency and constant phase difference.
- **Trap:** Two separately driven dippers automatically give a steady pattern. **Why it's wrong:** Their relative phase can drift. **Instead:** Drive both from one motor.
- **Trap:** Doubling slit separation doubles fringe spacing. **Why it's wrong:** $x$ is inversely proportional to $a$. **Instead:** Halve $x$ when $a$ doubles.
- **Trap:** Substitute millimetres beside metres. **Why it's wrong:** Mixed units create powers-of-ten errors. **Instead:** Convert every length to metres first.

## Exam technique
For **describe** questions, name the shared source, the two emitters, the detector or screen, and the observed alternating maxima and minima. For **explain**, link the shared drive to a constant phase difference and fixed pattern. For **calculate**, write $\lambda=ax/D$, show SI conversion, substitute, and give a sensible number of significant figures. If a question asks for a dark fringe, check its path difference rather than using the fringe-spacing formula alone.
