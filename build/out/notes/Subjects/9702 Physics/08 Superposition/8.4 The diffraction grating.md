# 8.4 The diffraction grating
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A diffraction grating produces sharp maxima at angles fixed by its line spacing and the light's wavelength. Measure an angle for a known diffraction order to determine the wavelength.

## Key ideas
A diffraction grating has many equally spaced lines or slits. The distance $d$ between adjacent lines is the **grating spacing**. When light is incident normally, the central bright spot is the **zero-order maximum**, $n=0$. First-order maxima appear symmetrically on either side, followed by higher integer orders if geometry allows. The **diffraction angle** $\theta$ is measured from the central, straight-through direction to one maximum, never from one side of the pattern to the other.

Constructive interference from adjacent slits requires a path difference of $n\lambda$. That path difference is $d\sin\theta$, giving the diffraction grating equation

$$
d\sin\theta=n\lambda.
$$

Here $\lambda$ is wavelength and $n$ is a whole-number diffraction order. If a grating has $N$ lines per metre, $d=1/N$ metres. For a label in lines per millimetre, first multiply by 1000 to get lines per metre. For example, 300 lines per millimetre means $N=300000\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}$ and $d=3.33\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$.

Because $\sin\theta\le1$, a maximum is possible only if $n\lambda\le d$. The largest possible integer order is the whole-number part of $d/\lambda$. If the pattern is visible on both sides, the number of maxima is $2n_{\max}+1$, including the central maximum. Whether a flat screen actually catches every possible order depends on its size.

## Method
1. Direct a narrow monochromatic beam normally onto a grating of known line density. Place a screen beyond it, or observe the angular positions directly. Identify the central maximum and a matching pair of order-$n$ maxima. The structure and use of a spectrometer are not needed.
2. Measure the angle from the central direction to one maximum. If you measure the angle between the matching pair, halve it. With a flat screen, measure its distance $L$ from the grating and the transverse distance $y$ from centre to one maximum; then $\theta=\tan^{-1}(y/L)$.
3. Convert line density into spacing $d$ in metres. Calculate $\lambda=d\sin\theta/n$. Repeat using both sides and available orders, then average consistent values. State the wavelength in metres or nanometres with sensible significant figures.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A grating has 500 lines per millimetre. Its second-order maxima are 80° apart. Calculate the wavelength.
> The angle to one second-order maximum is $40^\circ$. The spacing is $d=1/(500000)=2.00\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$.
> $$
> \lambda=\frac{d\sin\theta}{n}=\frac{(2.00\times10^{-6})\sin40^\circ}{2}=6.43\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}.
> $$
> This is $643\,\mathrm{nm}$.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Use the angle between symmetric maxima as $\theta$. **Why it's wrong:** The equation uses the angle from the centre to one maximum. **Instead:** Halve the full angle first.
- **Trap:** Use $y/L$ as $\sin\theta$. **Why it's wrong:** Screen geometry gives $y/L=\tan\theta$. **Instead:** Find $\theta$ with inverse tangent, then take its sine.
- **Trap:** Treat 300 lines per millimetre as $d=300\,\mathrm{mm}$. **Why it's wrong:** Line density and spacing are reciprocals. **Instead:** Convert to lines per metre and invert.
- **Trap:** Count only the positive orders. **Why it's wrong:** The pattern also has negative orders and zero order. **Instead:** Count both sides and the centre.

## Exam technique
For **Calculate** or **Determine**, write the identified order, a one-sided angle, line spacing in metres, the equation, substitution and unit. For **Describe** how to determine wavelength, give the beam and grating arrangement, the angle or screen-distance measurements, the line-density conversion and the final equation. Distinguish a possible order from a spot captured by a particular screen.
