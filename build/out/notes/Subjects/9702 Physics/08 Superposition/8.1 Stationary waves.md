# 8.1 Stationary waves
> [!abstract] In one breath
> When waves of the same type overlap, their instantaneous displacements add. Two waves travelling in opposite directions can produce a fixed pattern of nodes and antinodes, whose spacing reveals wavelength.

## Key ideas
The **principle of superposition** says that when two or more waves of the same type meet at a point, the resultant displacement equals the algebraic sum of their individual displacements at that point. Add signed displacements, not amplitudes: a crest displacement of $+3$ mm and a trough displacement of $-2$ mm give $+1$ mm at that instant. The waves continue through one another.

A **stationary wave** forms when two progressive waves of the same type, frequency and similar amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose. Reflection commonly supplies the returning wave. The resulting pattern does not travel along the medium. A **node** is a fixed position of zero amplitude: its displacement is zero at all times. An **antinode** is a fixed position of maximum amplitude. At some instants every particle can pass through equilibrium; a zero displacement at one instant therefore does not prove a node.

A graphical construction makes the formation clear. Draw the incident and reflected wave profiles on the same position axis at one time. At each position add their signed vertical displacements to draw the resultant curve. Repeat a quarter of a period later. Positions where the sums remain zero are nodes; positions with the largest motion are antinodes. Particles within one interval between adjacent nodes oscillate in phase. Across a node, the next loop oscillates in antiphase.

## Method
1. To demonstrate a microwave stationary wave, aim a transmitter at a metal reflector. Move a detector between them and record regularly spaced signal minima or maxima. The incident and reflected microwaves superpose.
2. For a stretched string, drive one end at a suitable frequency while the returning wave reflects at the other end. Look for stable loops, with fixed nodes between them. For an air column, drive it with sound and vary frequency or column length until resonance. A closed end is a displacement node; an open end is a displacement antinode when end corrections are negligible. Fine powder in a horizontal tube gathers at displacement nodes, where air motion is least.
3. Mark several successive nodes or antinodes. The separation $d$ of adjacent positions of the same kind is $\lambda/2$, so $\lambda=2d$. Between a node and its nearest antinode the distance is $\lambda/4$. Measuring across several gaps reduces the effect of uncertain positions: divide the total distance by the number of gaps before doubling.

> [!example]- Worked example
> The first and fifth nodes on a string are $0.80$ m apart. There are four adjacent-node gaps, so each gap is $0.80/4=0.20$ m.
> $$\lambda=2(0.20)=0.40\ \mathrm{m}$$
> The fifth node is four intervals from the first, not five.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Add the two amplitudes whenever waves meet. **Why it's wrong:** Amplitude is a maximum; the waves may have displacements of opposite signs at that instant. **Instead:** Add signed, instantaneous displacements at the stated point.
- **Trap:** Call every zero crossing a node. **Why it's wrong:** An antinode also crosses equilibrium during each cycle. **Instead:** Identify a point that remains at zero displacement throughout the cycle.
- **Trap:** Treat adjacent nodes as one wavelength apart. **Why it's wrong:** They are half a wavelength apart. **Instead:** Count the gaps and use $\lambda=2d$.

## Exam technique
For **explain**, say that the two progressive waves overlap, travel in opposite directions and have the same frequency; connect their superposition to fixed nodes and antinodes. For **describe** an experiment, name the source and reflector, what is moved or adjusted, and what observation identifies the pattern. For **determine** wavelength, show the number of node or antinode intervals and keep the unit with the final answer.
