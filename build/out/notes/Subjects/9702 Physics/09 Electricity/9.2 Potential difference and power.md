# 9.2 Potential difference and power
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Potential difference tells you how much energy is transferred for each coulomb passing through a component. Power tells you how quickly that transfer happens.

## Key ideas
**Potential difference across a component** is the **energy transferred per unit charge**. If energy $W$ is transferred when charge $Q$ passes through the component, then
$$V=\frac{W}{Q}.$$
The unit is the volt: $1\ \mathrm{V}=1\ \mathrm{J\,C^{-1}}$. For instance, $6.0\ \mathrm{V}$ means $6.0\ \mathrm{J}$ is transferred for each coulomb passing through the component. Write *per unit charge* in a definition: merely saying “energy transferred by a unit charge” does not clearly state the ratio.

Rearrange the same equation to find energy or charge: $W=VQ$ and $Q=W/V$. Keep joules, coulombs and volts consistent. A charge of $4.0\ \mathrm{C}$ through a component at $3.0\ \mathrm{V}$ transfers $12\ \mathrm{J}$.

**Electrical power** is the rate of energy transfer, $P=W/t$. Since current is charge per second, $I=Q/t$, replacing $W$ by $VQ$ gives
$$P=\frac{VQ}{t}=VI.$$
This works for a component when $V$ is the p.d. across that component and $I$ is its current. The unit of power is the watt, $1\ \mathrm{W}=1\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$. If $V$ or $I$ varies with time, $VI$ gives the power at that instant.

For a resistor with $V=IR$, substitution gives two further useful forms:
$$P=I^2R\qquad\text{and}\qquad P=\frac{V^2}{R}.$$
Choose the version matching the quantities you know. The same resistor has the same $R$ when comparing two operating points, so $P\propto I^2$ and $P\propto V^2$. A current multiplied by $1.5$ therefore makes the power $1.5^2=2.25$ times as great. Conversely, a power increase of $50\%$ makes the current $\sqrt{1.5}$ times as great, provided the resistance stays fixed.

## Method
1. Identify the component and the energy, charge, p.d., current, resistance or time that belongs to it. Do not silently use the supply voltage for a different component.
2. For energy per charge, start with $V=W/Q$ and rearrange if needed. For power, use $P=VI$; if the component obeys $V=IR$, choose $I^2R$ or $V^2/R$ when those avoid an extra step.
3. Convert units first: for example, $40\ \mathrm{mA}=0.040\ \mathrm{A}$ and $1.0\ \mathrm{h}=3600\ \mathrm{s}$. Substitute with units, calculate, then check sensible significant figures.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A fixed $0.80\ \mathrm{\Omega}$ resistor dissipates $7.2\ \mathrm{W}$. Its power rises by $50\%$. Calculate the new current.
> The new power is $1.5\times7.2=10.8\ \mathrm{W}$.
> Use $P=I^2R$ because resistance is fixed.
> $$I=\sqrt{\frac{P}{R}}=\sqrt{\frac{10.8}{0.80}}=3.7\ \mathrm{A}.$$
> The current rises by about $22\%$, not $50\%$, because power depends on its square.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Calling p.d. “energy transferred by a unit charge.” **Why it's wrong:** This can sound like an energy, whereas voltage is a ratio. **Instead:** Say “energy transferred per unit charge.”
- **Trap:** Dividing energy by current to find voltage. **Why it's wrong:** Current is charge per second. **Instead:** Use $V=W/Q$, with charge in coulombs.
- **Trap:** Treating power as proportional to current for a fixed resistor. **Why it's wrong:** $P=I^2R$. **Instead:** Square the current ratio, or square-root the power ratio.
- **Trap:** Using $P=V^2/R$ to compare series resistors as though they had the same voltage. **Why it's wrong:** Series components share current, while their p.d.s can differ. **Instead:** Use the common current and $P=I^2R$.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, give the precise ratio in words. For **Calculate** or **Determine**, state the equation before substituting values and show the unit. For **Show that**, display the algebra and substitution so the stated result follows clearly. In a circuit question, label which voltage and current belong to the named component; this prevents applying a supply value to the wrong resistor.
