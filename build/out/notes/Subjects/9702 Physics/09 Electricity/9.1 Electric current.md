# 9.1 Electric current
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Electric current is a flow of charge carriers. Counting the charge that crosses a section gives $Q=It$; counting the carriers swept through a conductor gives $I=Anvq$.

## Key ideas

An electric current is **a flow of charge carriers**. In a metal, free electrons carry charge through the conductor. In an electrolyte, moving positive and negative ions can carry charge. An energy transfer can accompany a current, but energy itself is not the charge carrier. Neutrons are uncharged, so their motion alone cannot make an electric current.

The charge on a carrier is **quantised**: it is an integer multiple of the elementary charge, $e=1.60\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C$. A carrier can have $+e$, $-e$, $+2e$, $-3e$, and so on; zero is also an integer multiple. Check a proposed charge by dividing it by $e$. For example, $4.8\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C=3e$ is possible, but $4.0\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm C=2.5e$ is not. The sign tells you the type of charge and does not invalidate a value.

Current measures the rate at which charge crosses a fixed section. For constant current,
$$
Q=It,
$$
where $Q$ is charge in coulombs, $I$ is current in amperes and $t$ is time in seconds. Thus one coulomb is the charge transferred by a current of one ampere in one second. If $Q$ is known, $I=Q/t$; if a count $N$ of electrons is known, use the charge magnitude $Q=Ne$.

For a current-carrying conductor,
$$
I=Anvq.
$$
Here $A$ is cross-sectional area in $\mathrm{m^2}$, $n$ is the **number density of charge carriers** in $\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ (number per unit volume), $v$ is their average drift speed in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and $q$ is the magnitude of charge per carrier in coulombs. This equation gives the magnitude of current; electron drift is opposite to conventional current. In time $t$, carriers travel $vt$ and sweep out volume $Avt$. The number of carriers in that volume is $nAvt$, so $Q=nAvtq$. Dividing by $t$ yields $I=Anvq$.

## Method

1. Identify the section that charge crosses and whether the question gives a total charge, a carrier count, or a number density.
2. Convert time to seconds, area to square metres and any wire length to metres. A circular wire has $A=\pi d^2/4$; doubling diameter makes area four times larger.
3. Use $Q=It$ for total charge or $I=Anvq$ for drift. If the total carrier count $N$ in a length $L$ is given, $n=N/(AL)$, so $I=Nqv/L$: the unknown area cancels.
4. Check that your answer has the expected unit and sensible magnitude. At fixed current in the same material, $n$ and $q$ are fixed, so drift speed is inversely proportional to area.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A metal wire has $A=2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$, $n=8.0\times10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}$ and electron drift speed $1.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Calculate its current.
> State the equation: $I=Anvq$, with $q=e$.
> $$
> I=(2.0\times10^{-6})(8.0\times10^{28})(1.0\times10^{-4})(1.60\times10^{-19})=2.56\,\mathrm A.
> $$
> To two significant figures, $I=2.6\,\mathrm A$. The electron drift direction does not make the current magnitude negative.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Calling $n$ carriers per unit area. **Why it's wrong:** $nA$ alone has units of carriers per metre. **Instead:** Use carriers per unit volume; multiply by the swept volume $Avt$.
- **Trap:** Assuming twice the diameter means twice the area. **Why it's wrong:** Area depends on diameter squared. **Instead:** Square the diameter ratio before comparing drift speeds.
- **Trap:** Changing charge passing each section of a steady wire according to its width. **Why it's wrong:** Current is the same through consecutive sections. **Instead:** For equal time intervals, $Q=It$ gives equal charge, while drift speed adjusts.

## Exam technique

For **State** or **Define**, use “flow of charge carriers” and “number per unit volume” precisely. For **Calculate**, write the equation, substitute in SI units and give the unit. For **Show that** $I=Anvq$, explicitly write the swept volume, carrier count and charge before dividing by time. When testing quantisation, display the ratio to $e$ and check that it is an integer.
