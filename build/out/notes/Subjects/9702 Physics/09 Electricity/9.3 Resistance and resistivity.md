# 9.3 Resistance and resistivity
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Resistance compares potential difference across a component with current through it. Its value can depend on temperature, light, and the dimensions and material of a conductor.

## Key ideas
**Resistance** is the ratio of potential difference across a component to the current through it: $R=V/I$. Equivalently, $V=IR$. One ohm means that a potential difference of one volt produces a current of one ampere. The equation can calculate resistance at any operating point; it does not by itself say that resistance stays fixed.

**Ohm’s law** states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided its temperature remains constant. Therefore $V/I$ is constant under that condition. A metallic conductor at constant temperature has an $I$–$V$ characteristic that is a straight line through the origin. Put current $I$ on the vertical axis and potential difference $V$ on the horizontal axis; label both axes and the origin.

A **filament lamp** has a curved $I$–$V$ characteristic through the origin. As current increases, the filament temperature increases, so its resistance increases. The curve becomes less steep at larger positive or negative potential differences. At zero p.d. the filament still has resistance: zero p.d. and zero current do not mean zero resistance. For a **semiconductor diode**, forward current is tiny until a threshold p.d., after which it rises sharply. Reverse current is approximately zero over the usual range. Its graph is strongly asymmetric.

These are illustrative characteristics, not universal numerical values. The metal is straight through the origin; the lamp flattens symmetrically; the diode has negligible reverse current and a sharp forward rise.

**Resistivity** $\rho$ describes the material. For a uniform wire of length $L$ and cross-sectional area $A$, $R=\rho L/A$, or $\rho=RA/L$. Resistivity has unit $\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$. A longer wire has more resistance; a thicker wire has less. For a circular wire, $A=\pi d^2/4$. Doubling diameter makes area four times larger and resistance one quarter as large when material and length stay fixed.

A **light-dependent resistor (LDR)** has decreasing resistance as light intensity increases. An assumed negative temperature coefficient **thermistor** has decreasing resistance as temperature increases. These trends are opposite to the rise of a metal filament’s resistance as it heats. When reasoning about a circuit, first identify which component receives more light or heat, then work out its new resistance.

## Method
1. Mark the potential difference **across** and current **through** the same component. Rearrange $V=IR$ for the requested quantity.
2. For a wire, convert length and area to metres and square metres. If a diameter is given, calculate $A=\pi d^2/4$ before substituting into $R=\rho L/A$.
3. For a graph, read a pair of $V$ and $I$ values at the same point. Calculate the resistance there using $R=V/I$. The gradient of a curved $I$–$V$ graph is not that resistance.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A wire has $\rho=2.0\times10^{-8}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, $L=4.0\,\mathrm{m}$ and $A=2.0\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}$. Calculate its resistance.
> Use $R=\rho L/A$ because the dimensions and resistivity are given.
> $$R=\frac{(2.0\times10^{-8})(4.0)}{2.0\times10^{-6}}=0.040\,\Omega$$
> Check: this is a small resistance for a short metal wire with a relatively large area.

> [!example]- Worked example: stretching at constant volume
> A uniform wire is stretched uniformly to three times its original length, keeping its volume and resistivity unchanged. Compare both resistances at the same temperature.
> First use $A_1L_1=A_2L_2$: since $L_2=3L_1$, $A_2=A_1/3$.
> Then $R_2/R_1=(L_2/L_1)(A_1/A_2)=3\times3=9$.
> The resistance becomes nine times as large. With fixed volume $\mathcal{V}=AL$, $R=\rho L^2/\mathcal{V}$, so $R\propto L^2$ for unchanged resistivity. Counting only the extra length misses the smaller area.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Write $V=IR$ as the whole of Ohm’s law. **Why it's wrong:** The law also requires proportionality at constant temperature. **Instead:** State that condition explicitly.
- **Trap:** Treat a filament as a fixed resistor. **Why it's wrong:** Its rising temperature raises resistance. **Instead:** Use $V/I$ at the relevant operating point.
- **Trap:** Use diameter in place of area. **Why it's wrong:** Area is proportional to diameter squared. **Instead:** Calculate $A=\pi d^2/4$.
- **Trap:** Reverse the LDR or thermistor trend. **Why it's wrong:** Both named components have lower resistance under their specified increase. **Instead:** Write “more light → lower LDR resistance” and “hotter → lower thermistor resistance.”

## Exam technique
For **Define**, give the ratio and name both quantities. For **State** Ohm’s law, include constant temperature. For **Sketch**, show the origin, labelled axes and the distinctive shape on both sides where relevant. For **Explain**, name the heating and resulting resistance change. For **Calculate**, show the equation, substitution and an answer with units and sensible significant figures.
