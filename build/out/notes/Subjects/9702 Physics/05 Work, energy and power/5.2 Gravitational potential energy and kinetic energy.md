# 5.2 Gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Raising an object transfers energy into its gravitational potential energy store; the gain depends on weight and **vertical** rise. A moving object's kinetic energy depends on its mass and the **square** of its speed.

## Key ideas

In a uniform gravitational field, the change in gravitational potential energy is the work done against gravity. For a vertical rise $\Delta h$,

$$
\Delta E_P=mg\Delta h.
$$

Here $m$ is mass in kg, $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, and $\Delta h$ is signed vertical displacement in metres. A rise gives positive $\Delta E_P$; a fall gives negative $\Delta E_P$. Energy changes are measured in joules. A longer frictionless ramp leading to the same height does not change the gain. If a force of friction acts, extra work is needed, but the gravitational potential energy gain is still $mg\Delta h$.

Kinetic energy is energy an object has because it moves:

$$
E_K=\tfrac12mv^2,
$$

where $v$ is speed in $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. This uses the object's total speed, whether motion is horizontal, vertical, or in a curved path. Doubling speed at fixed mass makes kinetic energy four times as great; doubling mass at fixed speed doubles it.

## Method

1. **Derive gravitational potential energy.** Begin with work done $W=Fs$ for a force parallel to displacement. To raise a mass at constant speed, the lifting force balances its weight, so $F=mg$. The displacement against gravity is the vertical height $s=\Delta h$. Thus $W=mg\Delta h=\Delta E_P$.
2. **Calculate an energy change.** Convert mass, height, and speed to SI units. Use vertical height for $\Delta E_P$, even if the object travels along a slope. Give the sign if the question asks for a change, and state the unit J.
3. **Derive kinetic energy.** For constant resultant force parallel to displacement, use $F=ma$ and $W=Fs=mas$. The motion equation $v^2=u^2+2as$ gives $as=(v^2-u^2)/2$. Hence $W=\tfrac12m(v^2-u^2)$: the work equals the change in kinetic energy. With $u=0$, the kinetic energy at speed $v$ is $\tfrac12mv^2$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A $0.40\,\mathrm{kg}$ projectile travels $18\,\mathrm{m}$ horizontally in $1.5\,\mathrm{s}$. Find its kinetic energy at maximum height if air resistance is negligible.
> At maximum height its vertical velocity is zero, but horizontal velocity remains $v_x=18/1.5=12\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
> $$
> E_K=\tfrac12(0.40)(12)^2=28.8\,\mathrm J\approx29\,\mathrm J.
> $$
> The horizontal motion is why the energy is not zero.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Substitute the length of a ramp for $\Delta h$. **Why it's wrong:** Gravity acts vertically. **Instead:** Find the vertical rise, then multiply it by weight.
- **Trap:** Say a falling object at constant speed gains kinetic energy. **Why it's wrong:** Its speed, hence $E_K$, is unchanged. **Instead:** Its gravitational energy loss is transferred through resistive forces to the surroundings.
- **Trap:** Put $\tfrac12$ into $W=Fs$. **Why it's wrong:** Work for a constant parallel force is $Fs$. **Instead:** The half appears when the motion equation is rearranged for displacement.
- **Trap:** Say kinetic energy is zero at a projectile's highest point. **Why it's wrong:** Horizontal velocity can remain. **Instead:** Use the total speed at that point.

## Exam technique

For **show that** or **derive**, write the starting equations and the substitution that produces the result. For a numerical **calculate** question, state the relevant energy equation, substitute SI values, and finish with J and sensible significant figures. If a question says “rate of loss” of gravitational potential energy during a steady fall, use the vertical distance travelled per second: $\Delta h/t=v$, so the positive loss rate is $mgv$.
