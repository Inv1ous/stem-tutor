# 5.1 Energy conservation
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A force transfers energy by doing work when an object is displaced in the force's direction. Energy is conserved across all stores and transfers; power tells us how quickly work is done, and efficiency tells us what fraction becomes useful output.

## Key ideas

**Work done** by a force is the force multiplied by the displacement in the direction of that force. If the force makes an angle $\theta$ with displacement $s$, its component along the motion is $F\cos\theta$, so

$$W=Fs\cos\theta.$$

Work and energy are measured in joules: $1\ \mathrm{J}=1\ \mathrm{N\,m}$. A force at right angles to displacement does no work. A force opposing displacement does negative work on the object; the positive *work done against resistance* is the energy transferred to thermal stores.

The **principle of conservation of energy** says that energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can be transferred between stores or transformed, while the total energy of a closed system remains constant. When an object slows or rises, compare its initial and final kinetic and gravitational potential energies, then account for transfers to the surroundings. At constant speed its kinetic energy stays the same, even if energy continues to flow. A falling object at terminal speed loses gravitational potential energy, which is transferred mainly to thermal energy of the object and air.

**Efficiency** is the ratio of useful energy output to total energy input:

$$\eta=\frac{E_{\mathrm{useful}}}{E_{\mathrm{input}}}=\frac{P_{\mathrm{useful}}}{P_{\mathrm{input}}}.$$

The power ratio applies when the powers describe the same time interval. Efficiency is a decimal from 0 to 1, or multiply by $100$ to give a percentage. Energy input is useful output plus wasted output. To find an unknown input, divide useful output by the decimal efficiency.

**Power** is work done per unit time (equivalently, energy transferred per unit time). Average power is $P=W/t$, in watts, where $1\ \mathrm{W}=1\ \mathrm{J\,s^{-1}}$. If force is along the displacement, $W=Fs$ and $v=s/t$, giving $P=(Fs)/t=Fv$. More generally, use the force component along velocity: $P=Fv\cos\theta$. The power of an engine uses its *driving force*; the resultant force instead gives the rate of change of kinetic energy.

## Method

1. Name the system and list its initial and final energy stores. Include useful transfers and losses such as heating by friction.
2. For work, identify the displacement **along the force**. Resolve the force with $\cos\theta$ if necessary, then use $W=Fs\cos\theta$.
3. Write an energy balance before inserting numbers. For example, initial kinetic energy plus lost gravitational potential energy equals final kinetic energy plus energy transferred by resistance.
4. For efficiency, turn a percentage into a decimal and write useful output over total input. Rearrange the ratio before substitution.
5. For power, identify the requested force or energy transfer, convert time to seconds, and choose $P=W/t$ or $P=Fv$. On a steady climb, driving force balances resistance plus the component of weight down the slope, $W\sin\theta$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A motor has useful output power $120\ \mathrm{W}$ and efficiency $60\%$. Find its input and wasted power.
> Write $\eta=P_{\mathrm{useful}}/P_{\mathrm{input}}$ and convert $60\%=0.60$.
> $$P_{\mathrm{input}}=\frac{120}{0.60}=200\ \mathrm{W}.$$
> The wasted power is $200-120=80\ \mathrm{W}$. Check that useful plus wasted output equals input.

> [!example]- Worked example: braking energy lifts a load
> A $240\,\mathrm{kg}$ cart stops from $5.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ on a level track. A mechanism transfers $65\%$ of its lost kinetic energy into the gravitational potential energy of a separate load raised $2.0\,\mathrm{m}$. The load starts and ends at rest. Calculate the load mass.
> Start with $E_{\mathrm{useful}}=\eta E_{\mathrm{input}}$.
> The input is the cart's lost kinetic energy; the useful output is the load's gravitational potential energy gain. Thus $mgh=\eta(\tfrac12 Mv^2)$, where $M$ is the cart mass and $m$ is the load mass.
> Input energy is $\tfrac12(240)(5.0)^2=3000\,\mathrm{J}$; useful energy is $0.65(3000)=1950\,\mathrm{J}$.
> Using $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, $m=1950/(9.81\times2.0)=99.4\,\mathrm{kg}\approx99\,\mathrm{kg}$ to two significant figures.
> The remaining $1050\,\mathrm{J}$ is transferred elsewhere, including heating; it is not destroyed.

**Self-check:** Which energy is the input to this mechanism, and why must the two masses remain distinct?

> [!answer]- Check your reasoning
> The input is the kinetic energy lost by the cart, not the gravitational energy gained by the load. The cart and load are different objects, so their masses cannot cancel.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Using the whole displacement when the force acts at an angle. **Why it's wrong:** Only the component parallel to motion does work. **Instead:** Use $Fs\cos\theta$.
- **Trap:** Saying constant speed means no energy transfer. **Why it's wrong:** Kinetic energy can remain constant while gravitational energy is transferred to heating. **Instead:** Track all stores and surroundings.
- **Trap:** Multiplying useful power by efficiency to find input. **Why it's wrong:** Useful output is already the smaller part. **Instead:** Divide by decimal efficiency.
- **Trap:** Using resultant force in $P=Fv$ for engine output. **Why it's wrong:** That gives net power. **Instead:** Use the engine's driving force.

## Exam technique

For **Define power**, write “work done per unit time”; $P=Fv$ is a derived relationship, not the definition. For **Show that $P=Fv$**, write $P=W/t$, $W=Fs$ for aligned force and displacement, then $s/t=v$. Include the force balance on slopes before substituting into $P=Fv$, and report sensible significant figures with units.
