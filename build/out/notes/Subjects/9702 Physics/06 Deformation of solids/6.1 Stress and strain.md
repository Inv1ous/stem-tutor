# 6.1 Stress and strain
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A tensile force pulls a specimen longer; a compressive force pushes it shorter. Within the limit of proportionality, load and extension have a constant ratio, allowing stiffness and Young modulus to be found from measurements.

## Key ideas
A **load** is the force applied to a specimen. **Extension** is the increase in length from its original, unloaded length: $x=L_{\mathrm{loaded}}-L$. **Compression** is a decrease in length. Here the forces and deformations act in one dimension. Tension pulls outward along the specimen; compression pushes inward.

**Hooke’s law** states that extension is directly proportional to applied force up to the **limit of proportionality**. That limit is the end of the straight-line region through the origin on a force–extension graph. It need not be the elastic limit: a specimen can sometimes recover its length after proportionality has ended. For one spring in its proportional region, $F=kx$, where the spring constant $k=F/x$ has unit $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$. A larger $k$ means a stiffer spring. On a graph of force against extension, the gradient is $k$; reversing the axes gives $1/k$.

**Tensile stress** is tensile force per unit cross-sectional area perpendicular to that force: $\sigma=F/A$. Its unit is the pascal, $\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. **Strain** is extension divided by *original* length: $\varepsilon=x/L$. It has no unit. The **Young modulus** is stress divided by strain in the proportional region:

$$
E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}=\frac{FL}{Ax}.
$$

Its unit is Pa. For a circular wire of diameter $d$, $A=\pi d^2/4$. Thus, at the same force and material, $x=FL/(AE)$: a longer wire extends more, while a thicker wire extends less.

## Method
1. To find $k$, identify a load and its *change* in length, convert that change to metres, then use $k=F/x$. If given two loaded readings, use the differences in force and extension.
2. To find $E$ for a wire, use $E=FL/(Ax)$ or first find stress and strain. Convert diameter, extension and original gauge length to metres before substitution.
3. In an experiment, clamp a long metal test wire beside an unloaded reference wire. Measure the original gauge length $L$. Use a micrometer to measure diameter at several positions and orientations; average the readings and calculate $A$. Add known loads gradually, recording the initial and loaded pointer positions to obtain each extension. A reference wire helps remove apparent extension caused by support movement. Plot force against extension and use the straight-line gradient $F/x$; then $E=(L/A)(F/x)$. Keep loads within the proportional region.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A wire has $L=2.0$ m and diameter $d=1.0$ mm. A force of $40$ N extends it by $1.0$ mm in the proportional region. Find $E$.
> Convert $d=x=0.0010$ m and calculate $A=\pi(0.0010)^2/4$.
> $$
> E=\frac{FL}{Ax}=\frac{40(2.0)}{[\pi(0.0010)^2/4](0.0010)}=1.02\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{Pa}.
> $$
> The original length belongs in the numerator; extension and cross-sectional area belong in the denominator.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Use the total loaded length as extension. **Why it's wrong:** Extension is a difference from the original length. **Instead:** Subtract the initial reading.
- **Trap:** Treat the limit of proportionality as automatically the elastic limit. **Why it's wrong:** Loss of proportionality and onset of permanent deformation are distinct. **Instead:** Identify the end of the straight graph segment.
- **Trap:** Double diameter and double area. **Why it's wrong:** Area depends on $d^2$. **Instead:** Recalculate $A=\pi d^2/4$.
- **Trap:** Use centimetres or millimetres directly in an SI result. **Why it's wrong:** This changes $k$ or $E$ by powers of ten. **Instead:** Convert every length to metres first.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, say “cross-sectional area” for stress and “original length” for strain. For **Describe** of the wire experiment, name each measurement, how extension is obtained from two readings, and how the graph gradient gives $E$. On a force–extension graph, state in words that the gradient is the spring constant; writing only $k$ may not earn the explanation mark.
