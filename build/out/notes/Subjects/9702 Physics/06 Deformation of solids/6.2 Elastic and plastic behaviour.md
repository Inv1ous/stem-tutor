# 6.2 Elastic and plastic behaviour
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Elastic deformation is reversed when a load is removed; plastic deformation leaves a permanent change. The area under a force–extension graph is the work done, and for a spring stretched within its limit of proportionality this work is stored as elastic potential energy.

## Key ideas

**Elastic deformation** is deformation reversed when the deforming force is removed: the object returns to its original dimensions. **Plastic deformation** is not completely reversed; a permanent change remains. An object may show both: if a compressed cylinder partly recovers when unloaded but stays shorter than at first, the recovery is elastic and the remaining shortening is plastic.

The **elastic limit** is the largest force or stress before plastic deformation begins. The **limit of proportionality** is where the straight-line part of a force–extension graph ends. These are different tests. A material can cease to obey Hooke’s law yet still recover its original dimensions when unloaded. A curved force–extension line alone does not prove plastic behaviour.

When an applied force changes a specimen’s extension by a small amount $\mathrm{d}x$, the work done is $F\,\mathrm{d}x$. Add these strips to get the **area under the force–extension graph** between the starting and finishing extensions. The units confirm the idea: $\mathrm{N\,m}=\mathrm{J}$. For a reversible elastic loading path, the work done increases the material’s elastic potential energy. Work done on a sample after it has passed its elastic limit is not necessarily all recoverable energy.

![[Assets/9702/9702-6.2-force-extension.svg]]

Within the limit of proportionality, $F=kx$. The graph from zero extension is a triangle, so

$$
E_P=\frac12Fx=\frac12kx^2.
$$

Here $x$ is **extension or compression from the natural length**, not the final length. The force $F$ is the force at that extension, not a constant force throughout the stretch. If force and extension change along a straight segment from $(x_1,F_1)$ to $(x_2,F_2)$, the added energy is the trapezium area $\tfrac12(F_1+F_2)(x_2-x_1)$.

## Method

1. Read the axes and identify the original and final states. If the graph shows total length, calculate extension as final length minus natural length.
2. Convert extension to metres before calculating an area or substituting into $E_P=\tfrac12kx^2$ with $k$ in $\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}$.
3. For a straight graph from the origin, use the triangle area $\tfrac12Fx$. For an interval that starts at nonzero extension, use the area **between** its endpoints, often a trapezium.
4. State whether the quantity is work input, recoverable elastic energy, or energy dissipated. This depends on the loading and unloading paths.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A linear force–extension graph shows 20 N at 1.0 mm and 50 N at 4.0 mm. Find the extra elastic energy stored.
> The width of the required area is $\Delta x=(4.0-1.0)\,\mathrm{mm}=0.0030\,\mathrm{m}$.
> The area is a trapezium, so
> $$
> \Delta E_P=\frac12(20+50)(0.0030)=0.105\,\mathrm{J}.
> $$
> Starting the area at zero would include energy already stored before the change.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Calling any nonlinear graph plastic. **Why it's wrong:** elastic recovery can persist beyond the limit of proportionality. **Instead:** judge plasticity by whether a permanent change remains after unloading.
- **Trap:** Using $Fx$ for a spring obeying Hooke’s law. **Why it's wrong:** the force rises from zero. **Instead:** use the average force $F/2$, giving $\tfrac12Fx$.
- **Trap:** Using a final length or a value in centimetres as $x$. **Why it's wrong:** $x$ is the change from natural length, expressed in metres for SI units. **Instead:** subtract the natural length and convert units before squaring.
- **Trap:** Calling the area between rubber loading and unloading curves stored energy. **Why it's wrong:** the unloading area is the returned work; the difference is dissipated as thermal energy. **Instead:** compare the two areas.

## Exam technique

For *define*, say what happens **after the force is removed**. For *calculate* or *determine*, show the graph region used, the conversion to metres, the equation, substitution and answer in joules. Check axis order when a paper plots extension vertically and force horizontally: work is still $\int F\,\mathrm{d}x$, so choose the region representing force multiplied by the change in extension.
