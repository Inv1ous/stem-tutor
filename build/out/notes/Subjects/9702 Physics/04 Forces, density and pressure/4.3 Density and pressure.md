# 4.3 Density and pressure
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Density tells us how much mass occupies a volume; pressure tells us how much normal force acts on each unit of area. In a fluid, pressure increases with depth, and the resulting difference in pressure forces produces upthrust.

## Key ideas

**Density** is mass per unit volume: $\rho=m/V$. Its SI unit is $\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Rearrange to $m=\rho V$ or $V=m/\rho$. For a mixture whose component volumes add, calculate each volume from its mass and density, then divide the total mass by the total volume. An ordinary average of component densities need not give the mixture density.

**Pressure** is normal force per unit area: $p=F/A$. The force must act perpendicular to the area. Its SI unit is the pascal, $\mathrm{Pa}=\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}$. A block resting on a table exerts a force equal to its weight $mg$ if there are no other vertical forces, so $p=mg/A$. The contact area matters: the same force over a smaller area produces a larger pressure.

In a stationary liquid of uniform density, the **hydrostatic pressure difference** between two points a vertical distance $\Delta h$ apart is $\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$. Here $\rho$ is the *fluid* density and $g=9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. Pressure at the deeper point is greater. This equation gives a difference, so it does not automatically give the total pressure: when a liquid surface is open to air, $p=p_{\mathrm{atm}}+\rho gh$ at depth $h$.

**Upthrust** is the upward force a fluid exerts on an immersed object. Its lower face is deeper, so the upward pressure force there exceeds the downward pressure force on its upper face. For a vertical block of constant cross-sectional area $A$, $U=(p_b-p_t)A$. Weight acts separately: the net upward force is $U-W$ when no other vertical forces act.

Archimedes’ principle states that upthrust equals the weight of fluid displaced: $U=\rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}gV_{\mathrm{displaced}}$. For a completely submerged object, the displaced volume is the whole object volume; for a partly submerged one, it is only the volume below the surface. If an object floats at rest, $U=W$, so $\rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}gV_{\mathrm{sub}}=mg$. The fraction *above* the surface is $1-V_{\mathrm{sub}}/V_{\mathrm{object}}$.

## Method

1. For density or pressure, identify the mass and volume or the normal force and contact area. Convert all quantities to SI units before dividing.
2. To **derive** the hydrostatic equation, take a liquid column of area $A$ and vertical height $\Delta h$. Its volume is $A\Delta h$, its mass is $\rho A\Delta h$, and its weight is $\rho A\Delta h g$. Divide weight by area: $\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$.
3. To **use** the equation, identify the vertical separation, use the fluid density, and decide whether pressure rises or falls between the specified points.
4. For upthrust, use either the difference of pressure forces or the weight of displaced fluid. If finding a balance or resultant force, include the object’s weight separately.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A block displaces $1.50\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}$ of water of density $1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}$. Calculate its upthrust.
> State $U=\rho_{\mathrm{fluid}}gV_{\mathrm{displaced}}$.
> Substitute $U=1000\times9.81\times1.50\times10^{-3}$.
> Hence $U=14.7\,\mathrm{N}$ to three significant figures.

## Traps

- **Trap:** Use the object’s density in $\Delta p=\rho g\Delta h$ or $U=\rho gV$. **Why it's wrong:** Both equations describe forces due to the fluid. **Instead:** Use the fluid density.
- **Trap:** Treat $\rho g\Delta h$ as a total pressure. **Why it's wrong:** It measures the change between levels. **Instead:** Add a known surface pressure if absolute pressure is requested.
- **Trap:** Subtract weight when asked for upthrust. **Why it's wrong:** $U-W$ is the resultant upward force. **Instead:** Calculate the fluid force first.
- **Trap:** Use total object volume for a partly submerged object. **Why it's wrong:** Only the submerged part displaces fluid. **Instead:** Use $V_{\mathrm{displaced}}$.

## Exam technique

For **Define**, give “mass per unit volume” or “normal force per unit area” exactly. For **Show (that)**, write every link in the column argument, including $V=A\Delta h$ and $m=\rho V$. For **Calculate**, show the equation, substitution, unit and sensible significant figures. In a manometer, first decide which side has higher pressure; in a floating question, check whether the requested percentage is above or below the surface.
