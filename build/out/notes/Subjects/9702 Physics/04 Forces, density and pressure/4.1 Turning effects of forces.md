# 4.1 Turning effects of forces
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A force can turn an object because its line of action misses a pivot. Two separated equal and opposite forces form a couple: they cancel as forces, yet their turning effects add.

## Key ideas
The **centre of gravity** is the single point where the whole weight of an object **may be taken to act**. Weight is distributed through the object; this point is a useful representation of its overall effect. For a uniform, symmetric object in a uniform gravitational field, it lies at the geometric centre. For example, the weight of a uniform rectangular sign hinged along its upper edge may be represented at the midpoint, not its lower edge. Locate this point before finding the weight's moment.

The **moment of a force about a point** is the product of the force and the *perpendicular distance* from that point to the force's line of action: $M=Fd_\perp$. Its unit is $\mathrm{N\,m}$. If the force makes angle $\theta$ with a lever of length $r$, then $d_\perp=r\sin\theta$, so $M=Fr\sin\theta$. A force whose line of action passes through the pivot has zero moment. State whether a nonzero moment is clockwise or anticlockwise when the situation requires direction.

A **couple** is a pair of equal, opposite, parallel forces acting along different lines. Their resultant force is zero, so they produce rotation only, rather than translation. The two forces' moments act in the same rotational sense. The **torque of a couple** is the product of the magnitude of *one* force and the perpendicular distance between their lines of action: $\tau=Fd$. The distance $d$ is the full separation. A couple's torque is independent of the pivot chosen.

## Method
1. Mark the pivot and draw or imagine each force's complete line of action. For weight, first locate the centre of gravity.
2. Measure the shortest, perpendicular distance from the pivot to that line. Convert centimetres to metres.
3. Multiply force by this distance. For an angled force, use $Fr\sin\theta$ if $\theta$ is between the lever and force.
4. For a couple, check equal magnitude, opposite direction and separated lines. Multiply one force by the full perpendicular separation. Give magnitude, unit and rotational sense where requested.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A uniform horizontal beam of length $0.80\,\mathrm{m}$ and mass $2.0\,\mathrm{kg}$ is hinged at one end. Calculate the moment of its weight about the hinge.
> Its centre of gravity is halfway along the beam, so $d_\perp=0.40\,\mathrm{m}$.
> $$M=mgd_\perp=(2.0)(9.81)(0.40)=7.8\,\mathrm{N\,m}.$$
> The weight acts downwards at the midpoint; the moment is in the sense that lowers the free end.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Say all the weight actually acts at the centre of gravity. **Why it's wrong:** Weight is distributed. **Instead:** Say it *may be taken to act* there.
- **Trap:** Use the distance to the point where a slanted force is applied. **Why it's wrong:** That length may not be perpendicular. **Instead:** Find the shortest distance to the force's line of action.
- **Trap:** Use the full length of a uniform sign hinged at its edge. **Why it's wrong:** Its centre of gravity is at the midpoint. **Instead:** Start with half its length, then project if inclined.
- **Trap:** Double $Fd$ for a couple. **Why it's wrong:** $d$ already spans both force lines. **Instead:** Use one force times the full separation.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, use “perpendicular distance” and “line of action” for moment, and “one force” with “distance between lines of action” for couple torque. For centre of gravity, write “may be taken to act”. For **Calculate**, show the equation, substitution, conversion and final $\mathrm{N\,m}$. On diagram questions, mark the pivot and draw the perpendicular before deciding between sine and cosine. If asked why a pair is a couple, identify both zero resultant force and nonzero turning effect.
