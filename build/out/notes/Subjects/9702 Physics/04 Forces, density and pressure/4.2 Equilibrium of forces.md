# 4.2 Equilibrium of forces
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Equilibrium means that an object has no resultant force and no resultant torque. You can test rotation with moments and translation with a closed vector triangle or by resolving forces.

## Key ideas
A moment is force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from its line of action to the chosen point. For an object in rotational equilibrium, **the sum of clockwise moments about a point equals the sum of anticlockwise moments about the same point**. This is the principle of moments. You may choose any convenient point, usually a hinge, but every moment in the equation must use that one point. A force acting through it has zero moment.

For full equilibrium of a rigid object, both $\Sigma\vec F=0$ and $\Sigma\tau=0$. A stationary object can have forces acting on it that cancel. An object can also move with constant velocity while in equilibrium. Zero resultant force alone does not prevent rotation: two equal and opposite parallel forces with separate lines of action form a couple and give a torque. Zero resultant torque alone does not prevent linear acceleration.

Three **coplanar** forces in equilibrium can be drawn as a vector triangle. Draw each arrow in its actual direction, head to tail, using one scale. The last arrow returns to the first arrow's tail. Closure means the vector sum is zero. The triangle tests force balance; if a rigid body is involved, check torque balance too.

## Method
1. Draw a free-body diagram of the chosen object. Mark the direction and line of action of every force. For a uniform rod, place its weight at the midpoint.
2. Choose a moment centre that removes an unknown reaction. Write clockwise and anticlockwise sums using perpendicular distances. If a force makes an angle $\theta$ with a horizontal rod, its turning component is $F\sin\theta$.
3. Resolve forces horizontally and vertically to find reactions or unknown tensions. Both component sums must be zero.
4. For three coplanar forces, arrange their vectors head to tail. Use the triangle's angles with trigonometry, then check that it closes.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A uniform horizontal rod weighs 18 N, is hinged at one end, and is supported by an upward force $F$ at the other end. Its length is 2.0 m.
> The rod's weight acts 1.0 m from the hinge. Taking moments about the hinge gives
> $$F(2.0)=18(1.0).$$
> Hence $F=9.0$ N. The hinge's force produces no moment about the hinge, but it may still be needed when balancing vertical forces.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Put a uniform beam's whole weight at its far end. **Why it's wrong:** Its centre of mass is halfway along. **Instead:** Use the midpoint as the weight's line of action.
- **Trap:** Balance clockwise moments about one point against anticlockwise moments about another. **Why it's wrong:** Those sums do not test zero torque about a common point. **Instead:** Name one moment centre before writing the equation.
- **Trap:** Use one string's vertical component for a load supported symmetrically by two strings. **Why it's wrong:** Both strings lift the load. **Instead:** Write $2T\sin\theta=W$ when $\theta$ is measured from the horizontal.
- **Trap:** Assume a closed force triangle proves full equilibrium. **Why it's wrong:** A separate torque may remain. **Instead:** Check resultant torque too.

## Exam technique
For **State**, give the complete principle: rotational equilibrium, sums, clockwise and anticlockwise, and the **same point**. For **Calculate**, state the moment or component equation before substitution, carry the correct perpendicular distance, and finish with a unit. For a vector triangle, preserve arrow directions and label the angle to the vertical or horizontal before choosing sine or cosine. A force in newtons and a moment in newton metres cannot be interchanged.
