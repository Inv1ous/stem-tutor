# 10.2 Kirchhoff’s laws
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Charge is conserved at a junction and energy is conserved around a closed loop. Those two facts let us derive resistance rules and solve circuits even when current splits.

## Key ideas
**Kirchhoff’s first law** states that the sum of currents entering a junction equals the sum leaving it. Current is charge flowing per second. If more charge entered than left, charge would accumulate at the junction; in a steady circuit it does not. For example, if $I_1$ enters and $I_2$ and $I_3$ leave, $I_1=I_2+I_3$. This is the junction rule and expresses conservation of charge.

**Kirchhoff’s second law** states that, around a closed loop, the algebraic sum of electromotive forces equals the algebraic sum of potential differences. Equivalently, the signed changes in potential sum to zero. A charge returning to its starting point has no net change in energy, so this loop rule expresses conservation of energy. Crossing a cell from negative to positive gives an e.m.f. rise; crossing it the other way gives a fall. A resistor traversed in the direction of its current gives a p.d. drop $IR$.

**Resistors in series:** The same current $I$ passes through each resistor. The loop law gives $V=V_1+V_2=IR_1+IR_2$. Since $V=IR$ for the combination, cancel $I$ to obtain $R=R_1+R_2$. For two or more resistors in series, add every resistance: $R=R_1+R_2+\cdots$. Their p.d.s generally differ; the common quantity is current.

**Resistors in parallel:** Both ends of each branch connect to the same two junctions, so each branch has the same p.d. $V$. The junction law gives $I=I_1+I_2=V/R_1+V/R_2$. Since $I=V/R$ for the combination, divide by $V$ to obtain $1/R=1/R_1+1/R_2$. For two or more resistors in parallel, continue the reciprocal sum. For two, $R=R_1R_2/(R_1+R_2)$. The result must be smaller than either branch resistance: an extra path allows more current at fixed p.d.

## Method
1. Mark each assumed current direction and each cell polarity. A negative answer later means the actual current runs opposite to the assumed arrow.
2. Identify genuine junctions. Write one independent incoming-current-equals-outgoing-current equation at each needed junction.
3. Choose a direction around a closed loop. Count cell rises and resistor drops with signs; write the energy equation.
4. Simplify obvious series or parallel groups, then solve the equations. Substitute back into a junction and a loop equation as a check. Keep units consistent, especially $\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $\Omega$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A 12 V cell supplies a 2.0 $\Omega$ resistor in series with two 4.0 $\Omega$ resistors in parallel. Calculate the source current and each branch current.
> The parallel pair has $1/R_p=1/4.0+1/4.0$, so $R_p=2.0\,\Omega$.
> The series total is $R_T=2.0+2.0=4.0\,\Omega$. Around the source loop, $12=I R_T$, giving $I=3.0\,\mathrm{A}$.
> At the junction the two equal branches carry equal current, so each has $1.5\,\mathrm{A}$. Their currents sum to the source current.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Adding parallel resistances directly. **Why it's wrong:** The branch currents, rather than the branch p.d.s, add. **Instead:** Add reciprocals and check that the result is below the smallest branch resistance.
- **Trap:** Adding every cell e.m.f. as positive. **Why it's wrong:** A reversed cell opposes the others. **Instead:** Mark cell polarities and assign each rise or fall a sign around the chosen loop.
- **Trap:** Calling a junction equation the second law. **Why it's wrong:** The first law concerns charge flow at a junction. **Instead:** Use the second law only around a closed loop, where energy per charge balances.

## Exam technique
For **Show that** or a derivation, start from the physical equalities: common current and summed p.d.s in series; common p.d. and summed currents in parallel. Write $V=IR$ for the equivalent resistance before cancelling. In numerical circuit questions, draw the current arrows and retain signed e.m.f.s. A final current or p.d. without the conservation equation may miss a method mark. Check a parallel answer against the smallest branch and verify your currents sum at every junction.
