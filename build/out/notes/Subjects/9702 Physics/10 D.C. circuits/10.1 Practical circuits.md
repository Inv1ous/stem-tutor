# 10.1 Practical circuits
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A source gives energy to each coulomb of charge as it drives it around a complete circuit. Some of that energy can be transferred inside the source, leaving less per coulomb available at its terminals.

## Key ideas
Learn the standard symbols in section 6 of the syllabus and use them consistently. A cell has one long and one short parallel line; a battery has multiple such pairs. A fixed resistor is a rectangle, and a variable resistor adds a diagonal arrow. An ammeter is a circle with A; a voltmeter is a circle with V. Recognise the prescribed symbols for switches, lamps, diodes, light-dependent resistors, thermistors and the oscilloscope as well. Draw straight wires and clear junctions. A crossing with no junction dot does not connect branches.

A circuit diagram records electrical connections, not the physical position of equipment on a bench. Trace a continuous loop from one source terminal to the other to decide whether current can flow. Components in series share a path and the same current. Components in parallel connect to the same two junctions and have the same potential difference. An ammeter must be in series with the component whose current it measures; a voltmeter connects across the two points whose p.d. it measures.

The electromotive force, or e.m.f., of a source is **energy transferred per unit charge in driving charge around a complete circuit**:
$$E=\frac{W}{Q}.$$
The unit is the volt, equivalent to a joule per coulomb. A battery marked 9.0 V supplies 9.0 J per coulomb to the whole circuit. That label does not promise 9.0 J per coulomb to the external components alone.

Potential difference across a component is **energy transferred from electrical energy per unit charge** in that component. For a resistor, this energy usually becomes thermal energy; for a lamp it can become light and thermal energy. E.m.f. describes energy supplied per charge by the source, whereas p.d. describes energy transferred per charge across a component. Both have the same unit even though they describe different energy transfers.

A real source has internal resistance $r$. When it supplies current $I$, energy per charge $Ir$ is dissipated internally. Its terminal p.d. is therefore
$$V=E-Ir.$$
The term $Ir$ is called the lost volts. At zero current, $Ir=0$ and terminal p.d. equals e.m.f. At greater current, lost volts rise and terminal p.d. falls if $E$ and $r$ stay fixed. If the external resistance rises, current often falls and terminal p.d. rises towards $E$.

## Method
1. Read the circuit topology: find the source, its internal resistance, junctions, and the path of current. Place an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the relevant two points.
2. For an energy question, use $E=W/Q$ or $W=EQ$ and include the **complete** circuit in your interpretation.
3. For terminal p.d., write $E=V+Ir$ first. Substitute with volts, amperes and ohms, then state the answer in volts. Check that a discharging cell with nonzero current has $V<E$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A cell has e.m.f. 12.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 $\Omega$. It supplies 2.0 A. Calculate its terminal p.d.
> Write $V=E-Ir$ because the internal p.d. uses some of the supplied energy per charge.
> $$V=12.0-(2.0)(0.50)=11.0\ \mathrm{V}.$$
> The result is below the e.m.f., as expected for a source supplying current.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Put the voltmeter in series. **Why it's wrong:** It must compare two points. **Instead:** Connect it across the component or source terminals.
- **Trap:** Treat a battery's e.m.f. as the external p.d. under load. **Why it's wrong:** Charge transfers energy inside the source. **Instead:** Subtract $Ir$.
- **Trap:** Say e.m.f. or p.d. is an amount of energy. **Why it's wrong:** Both are energy **per unit charge**. **Instead:** State the ratio and the energy transfer involved.

## Exam technique
For **Define**, include “energy transferred per unit charge” and the complete circuit. For **Explain** questions about changing terminal p.d., say how current changes, then how $Ir$ changes, then use $V=E-Ir$. In diagram questions, trace actual joined wires: nearby symbols or crossed wires do not automatically form a conducting path.
