# 10.3 Potential dividers
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A potential divider shares a supply potential difference (p.d.) between series components according to their resistances. A potentiometer uses the same idea along a uniform wire and locates equality of p.d.s by a zero galvanometer reading.

## Key ideas
A **potential divider** consists of resistances connected in series across a source. The same current flows through each, so $V=IR$ makes the p.d. across a component proportional to its resistance. For two resistances $R_1$ and $R_2$, with output across $R_2$,
$$
V_{out}=V_{in}\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}.
$$
Always identify the two output terminals first: changing which component the voltmeter spans reverses many predictions. If a load is connected across one arm, it is parallel with that arm; its effective resistance changes the divider ratio.

A **potentiometer** has a uniform resistance wire supplied with a steady current. Equal lengths have equal resistance and equal p.d., so the potential gradient $k=V_{wire}/L$ is constant. The p.d. along a length $l$ is $V=kl$. To compare two p.d.s without relying on an ordinary voltmeter reading, find their balance lengths $l_1$ and $l_2$ while keeping the wire current unchanged:
$$
\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{l_1}{l_2}.
$$
The unknown p.d. must lie within the range available along the wire. For cell e.m.f. measurements, connect its polarity so its e.m.f. opposes the chosen wire p.d.

A **galvanometer** in a null method detects the point of zero deflection. At this balance point, the opposed p.d.s are equal and no current passes through the comparison branch. The galvanometer need not have a scale calibrated in amperes. When the unknown is a cell, no current through it means no p.d. drop across its internal resistance, so its e.m.f. is measured directly.

An NTC **thermistor** loses resistance as temperature rises; an **LDR** loses resistance as light intensity rises. Put either sensor in a divider arm to produce a temperature-dependent or light-dependent output p.d. The output across the sensor falls when its resistance falls, if the other resistance and supply p.d. stay fixed. The output across the fixed resistor rises under the same change.

## Method
1. Mark the supply terminals, the output terminals and the series resistances.
2. Write $V_{out}/V_{in}=R_{out}/R_{total}$, using the resistance actually spanned by the output.
3. For a potentiometer, move the contact until the galvanometer reads zero. Use the measured balance length and the wire's potential gradient, or compare two balance lengths on the same wire.
4. For a sensor, decide how temperature or light changes its resistance before applying the divider ratio.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A $1.50$ V reference cell balances at $75.0$ cm on a uniform wire. An unknown cell balances at $45.0$ cm without changing the wire current. Find its e.m.f.
> At null, each cell e.m.f. equals the p.d. along its balance length. Thus
> $$
> \frac{E_x}{1.50}=\frac{45.0}{75.0}.
> $$
> Hence $E_x=0.900$ V. There is no current through the unknown cell at balance, so internal resistance causes no lost volts.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Treating the whole supply p.d. as the p.d. across one series resistor. **Why it's wrong:** The supply p.d. is shared. **Instead:** Use the resistance fraction for the selected output arm.
- **Trap:** Requiring zero internal resistance in the unknown cell. **Why it's wrong:** Current through it is zero at null. **Instead:** Check for zero galvanometer deflection.
- **Trap:** Assuming dimmer light lowers LDR resistance. **Why it's wrong:** An LDR's resistance rises in dimmer light. **Instead:** Establish the sensor resistance change first, then use the divider ratio.

## Exam technique
For **Calculate** questions, state the relevant ratio, substitute numbers, and give the p.d. or e.m.f. with units. For **Explain**, link the changed temperature or light intensity to sensor resistance, then to the output voltage fraction. In null questions, explicitly mention zero current and equality of opposing p.d.s. In diagrams, inspect polarity and which section of wire or resistor the stated p.d. spans.
