# 1.4 Scalars and vectors
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A scalar is a size; a vector is a size **and** a direction, so vectors must be added, subtracted and resolved with geometry, not with ordinary arithmetic. Every vector problem reduces to right-angled triangles: build the triangle, then use Pythagoras and $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$.

## Key ideas

**Scalar quantity**: has magnitude only. **Vector quantity**: has magnitude and direction.

- Scalars: distance, speed, mass, time, energy, work, power, temperature, density, pressure.
- Vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum, field strength.

A sign is not a direction: a temperature of $-5\,^\circ\mathrm{C}$ is still a scalar. A scalar can be built from vectors (work is force times displacement in the direction of the force).

**Resultant vector**: the single vector with the same effect as two or more vectors acting together (their vector sum). Vectors that lie in the same plane are **coplanar**.

**Adding**: draw the vectors to scale head to tail (a vector triangle); the resultant runs from the tail of the first to the head of the last. Two vectors that are perpendicular give a right-angled triangle, so $R=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.

**Subtracting**: $\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{X}+(-\mathbf{Y})$, where $-\mathbf{Y}$ has the same size as $\mathbf{Y}$ but the opposite direction. A change is always final minus initial:
$$\Delta\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{f}-\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{i}$$

![[Assets/9702/9702-1.4-velocity-change.svg]]

**Resolving**: any vector $F$ at angle $\theta$ to the $x$-axis is equivalent to two perpendicular components. Perpendicular components do not affect each other, so treat each direction separately.
$$F_x=F\cos\theta,\qquad F_y=F\sin\theta,\qquad F=\sqrt{F_x^2+F_y^2},\qquad \tan\theta=\frac{F_y}{F_x}$$

![[Assets/9702/9702-1.4-components.svg]]

## Method

1. **Sketch** each vector as an arrow with its direction and size labelled.
2. **Choose axes** (along a slope, or horizontal and vertical) and a positive direction for each.
3. **Adding or subtracting**: resolve every vector into $x$ and $y$ components, add the components in each direction (with signs), then combine with $R=\sqrt{R_x^2+R_y^2}$.
4. **Direction**: give $\tan\theta=R_y/R_x$ and state the angle from a named line ("$37^\circ$ below the leftward horizontal").
5. **Check**: the resultant of two vectors is never larger than the sum of their magnitudes.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A boat is steered at $3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ directly across a river flowing at $1.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Determine the velocity relative to the bank.
>
> 1. Sketch the vector triangle head to tail: the two velocities are perpendicular.
>    *(Perpendicular vectors give a right-angled triangle, so Pythagoras applies.)*
> 2. Magnitude:
> $$v=\sqrt{3.0^2+1.2^2}=3.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$$
>    *(Larger than either velocity but smaller than their sum, $4.2\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.)*
> 3. Direction:
> $$\tan\theta=\frac{3.0}{1.2}\ \Rightarrow\ \theta=68^\circ \text{ to the bank, downstream}$$
>    *(A vector answer needs a direction relative to a named reference.)*

## Traps

- **Trap:** Calling temperature, charge or time a vector because it has a sign or "goes forwards". **Why it's wrong:** a sign is not a direction in space. **Instead:** ask "would I need to say which way, in space?" If not, it is a scalar.
- **Trap:** Calling upthrust a scalar, or pressure, work and energy vectors. **Why it's wrong:** upthrust is a force, so a vector; pressure, work and energy have no direction. **Instead:** classify by the definition, not by whether forces are involved.
- **Trap:** Adding or subtracting magnitudes ($8-3=5$ for a bounce). **Why it's wrong:** the velocity reversed, so the change is $8+3=11\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Instead:** choose a positive direction and use signs.
- **Trap:** Treating $\Delta\mathbf{v}$ as $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{f}+\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{i}$. **Why it's wrong:** that is the resultant, not the change. **Instead:** reverse $\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{i}$ first, then add.
- **Trap:** Swapping $\sin$ and $\cos$. **Why it's wrong:** the component next to the angle is $F\cos\theta$. **Instead:** check the limit: at $\theta=0$ the component along the axis must be the whole $F$.

## Exam technique

- *Define* a vector: "magnitude **and** direction"; a scalar has magnitude only. Give an example if asked.
- *Determine* or *Calculate* a resultant: sketch the triangle, state the equation, substitute, and give the direction as well as the magnitude.
- For angles between forces, check which angle the question gives: the angle between the lines of action ($60^\circ$ between two forces) is not the angle to the horizontal.
- Two forces of equal size at $60^\circ$ have resultant $2F\cos30^\circ$, not $2F$.
- Give answers to 2 or 3 significant figures, with units.
