# 1.1 Physical quantities
> [!abstract] In one breath
> A physical quantity is something you can measure, and it always has a numerical magnitude and a unit. In estimation questions you pick sensible anchor values, convert to SI units and multiply, aiming for the right power of ten rather than exact digits.

## Key ideas
- **Physical quantity:** consists of a numerical magnitude and a unit, written $Q = \text{number}\times\text{unit}$, for example $v = 12\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
- **Quantity or unit?** Temperature, length, time, current and pressure are quantities. Kelvin, metre, second, ampere and pascal are units. Learn the pairs.
- **What a record needs:** a number and a unit. The unit does not have to be an SI unit or a base unit, and only vector quantities need a direction.
- **No unit, not a physical quantity:** strain, efficiency and atomic number are pure ratios or counts. Number density ($\mathrm{m^{-3}}$) does have a unit, so it counts. CAIE MCQs treat unit-less ratios as not physical quantities; in wider SI usage they are dimensionless quantities.
- **Changing unit changes the number, not the quantity:** $0.75\ \mathrm{m} = 750\ \mathrm{mm}$. A smaller unit needs a larger number.

## Method
Estimating a quantity:

1. Break the quantity into things you know: mass, speed, length, density.
2. Choose anchor values and write them down. Adult about 80 kg, sprinter about $10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, atom about $10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$ across.
3. Convert every value to SI units before using an equation. Convert lengths first, then raise to the power: $1\ \mathrm{cm^3} = (10^{-2})^3\ \mathrm{m^3} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. Divide $\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$ by 3.6.
4. State the equation, substitute, and evaluate.
5. Give one or two significant figures and check the size is sensible.

> [!example]- Worked example
> Estimate the number of atoms in a metal cube of side $3.0\ \mathrm{cm}$. Atomic diameter $1.0\times10^{-10}\ \mathrm{m}$.
>
> 1. Side in metres: $0.030\ \mathrm{m}$, so $V = (0.030)^3 = 2.7\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{m^3}$. Cubing the metre value avoids the $10^{-2}$ trap.
> 2. Volume of one atom: $(10^{-10})^3 = 10^{-30}\ \mathrm{m^3}$.
> 3. Number of atoms:
>
> $$N = \frac{V}{V_{atom}} = \frac{2.7\times10^{-5}}{10^{-30}} = 2.7\times10^{25}$$
>
> So there are about $3\times10^{25}$ atoms.

## Traps
- **Trap:** choosing kelvin, metre or pascal as a physical quantity. **Why it's wrong:** these are units, and the quantity is temperature, length or pressure. **Instead:** ask whether it is what is measured or what it is measured in.
- **Trap:** saying a record must have an SI or base unit. **Why it's wrong:** any correct unit, such as cm or km, gives a valid record. **Instead:** say numerical value and unit.
- **Trap:** saying all physical quantities have a direction. **Why it's wrong:** scalars such as mass and energy do not. **Instead:** magnitude and unit only.
- **Trap:** counting efficiency, strain or atomic number as physical quantities in a 9702 MCQ. **Why it's wrong:** they have no unit, and CAIE MCQs only accept quantities with a magnitude and a unit (in wider SI usage they are called dimensionless quantities). **Instead:** look for a unit.
- **Trap:** converting $\mathrm{cm^3}$ with $10^{-2}$, or using $\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}$ as $\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. **Why it's wrong:** it is a factor of $10^4$ out for $\mathrm{cm^3}$, and 3.6 out for a speed (about 13 out once it is squared in $E_k$). **Instead:** convert length first, then cube; divide by 3.6.

## Exam technique
- *Identify* questions test unit versus quantity: check that each option has both a magnitude and a unit.
- *Estimate* questions reward a sensible value and a clear route. State your anchors and the equation; aim for a sensible value with clear working, and make sure the answer is the right order of magnitude.
- *Explain* why something is not a physical quantity: say it is a unit, name the quantity, and note that a magnitude is also needed.
- Check the power of ten at the end. A person walking at $15\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ or a lorry of $5\times10^{2}\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ momentum should ring alarm bells.
