# 3.1 Momentum and Newton’s laws of motion
> [!abstract] In one breath
> Mass measures resistance to a change in motion. A resultant force changes an object's momentum and, for constant mass, accelerates it in the direction of that force.

## Key ideas
Mass is the property of an object that resists change in motion: its inertia. The same resultant force gives a smaller acceleration to a greater mass. Mass is measured in kg; it is not a force. Weight is the force on a mass due to a gravitational field. Its magnitude is $W=mg$, where $g=9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ near Earth's surface. Weight is in newtons and can change with gravitational field strength while mass stays the same.

Newton's first law states that an object remains at rest or continues at constant velocity in a straight line unless acted on by a resultant force. Zero resultant force means zero acceleration, even if the object is moving. Newton's second law states that resultant force equals the rate of change of momentum and acts in the direction of that change. For constant mass, this becomes $F_{\mathrm{net}}=ma$: acceleration and resultant force always point in the same direction. Add forces as vectors before using this equation; a driving force alone is usually not the resultant.

Linear momentum is the product of mass and velocity: $p=mv$. Its SI unit is $\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$. Momentum is a vector because velocity is a vector. The resultant force is the rate of change of momentum, $F_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta p/\Delta t$. Over an interval, use the average force and signed change in momentum. With constant mass, $\Delta p=m(v-u)$ and $F_{\mathrm{net}}=m(v-u)/\Delta t=ma$.

Newton's third law says that when two bodies interact, each exerts a force on the other of the same type and magnitude but opposite direction. These forces act on different bodies, so they do not cancel in a force balance for either body. For example, Earth's gravitational pull on a book is paired with the book's gravitational pull on Earth. The table's upward force on the book balances its weight only when the book has zero vertical acceleration; it is not the weight's third-law partner.

## Method
1. Choose the object and a positive direction. List only forces **on that object**, with signs or arrows. Find their resultant.
2. For constant mass, write $F_{\mathrm{net}}=ma$ and substitute SI quantities. A force opposite the chosen direction has a negative sign.
3. For a collision, label initial and final velocities with signs, calculate $\Delta p=m(v-u)$, then divide by contact time. State force direction if asked.
4. For gravitational force, use $W=mg$. If solving for mass, rearrange to $m=W/g$; do not substitute the object's actual acceleration for $g$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A $0.20\ \mathrm{kg}$ ball travels right at $5.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$ and rebounds left at $3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$. Contact lasts $0.10\ \mathrm{s}$. Find average force on the ball.
> Take right as positive: $u=+5.0$, $v=-3.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$.
> $$\Delta p=m(v-u)=0.20(-3.0-5.0)=-1.6\ \mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}$$
> $$F_{\mathrm{avg}}=\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}=\frac{-1.6}{0.10}=-16\ \mathrm{N}$$
> The average force is $16\ \mathrm{N}$ left, exerted by the wall on the ball.

## Traps
- **Trap:** Subtracting rebound speed magnitudes. **Why it's wrong:** The two velocities have opposite signs. **Instead:** Use signed $v-u$; a reversal adds the speed magnitudes in $|\Delta v|$.
- **Trap:** Equating thrust with $ma$ when weight or drag acts. **Why it's wrong:** The second law uses the resultant force. **Instead:** Write a signed force balance first.
- **Trap:** Calling the support force and weight a third-law pair. **Why it's wrong:** Both act on the same object and are different interactions. **Instead:** Match the same force type on different bodies.
- **Trap:** Inferring zero force from an instant of zero velocity. **Why it's wrong:** A ball can have zero velocity at its turning point while accelerating. **Instead:** Ask whether velocity is changing.

## Exam technique
*Define* momentum as the product of mass and velocity; *define* force as the rate of change of momentum. For *state* questions on the third law, explicitly say equal magnitude, opposite directions, same type and different bodies. In calculations, show the resultant force or signed momentum change before the final answer, convert grams to kilograms and minutes to seconds, and give units. A diagram of all forces on the selected body often prevents sign and third-law errors.
