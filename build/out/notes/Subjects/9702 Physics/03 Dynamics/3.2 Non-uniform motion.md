# 3.2 Non-uniform motion

> [!abstract] In one breath
> Friction and viscous (drag) forces always oppose motion, and a drag force grows from zero as the speed grows. So a falling object's acceleration shrinks from $9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ towards zero, and it ends up moving at a constant terminal velocity.

## Key ideas

**Frictional force** — a force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency to move) of two surfaces in contact.

**Viscous force / drag force** — a resistive force on a body moving through a fluid. It opposes the motion and **increases as the speed of the body increases**; it is zero when the body is at rest. **Air resistance** is the drag force due to air. (No coefficients of friction or viscosity are needed at AS; the simple model "drag grows with speed" is enough.)

**Upthrust** — the extra upward force on a body immersed in a fluid, equal to the weight of fluid displaced. It depends on volume and fluid density, *not* on speed, so it is constant for a fully immersed body in a uniform liquid. In air it is usually negligible.

**Terminal velocity** — the constant velocity a body reaches when the resistive force (plus any upthrust) becomes equal in magnitude to the weight, so the resultant force, and therefore the acceleration, is zero.

For a body falling in air, taking downwards as positive:

$$ma = W - D = mg - D$$

![[Assets/9702/9702-3.2-vt-fall.svg]]

At terminal velocity $a = 0$, so in air $D = mg$, and in a liquid $D + U = mg$.

![[Assets/9702/9702-3.2-terminal-forces.svg]]

Two consequences worth remembering:

- **Mass matters.** For two bodies of the same size and shape, the heavier one needs a bigger drag force to balance its weight, so it reaches a **larger** terminal velocity.
- **Energy.** At terminal velocity the kinetic energy is constant, so the gravitational potential energy lost becomes thermal energy (and kinetic energy) of the body and the surrounding air — not kinetic energy of the body.

### Horizontal motion: a car

For a car already moving forwards along a straight, level road, take forwards as positive. Here $F_\mathrm{friction}$ denotes the total backward resistance, including air resistance:

$$F_\mathrm{res}=F_\mathrm{drive}-F_\mathrm{friction}=ma.$$

- Driving force greater than friction: forward resultant and acceleration; the car speeds up.
- Equal forces: zero resultant and acceleration; the car continues at constant velocity.
- Driving force smaller than friction: backward resultant and acceleration; the car slows while still moving forwards.

For example, a $1200\ \mathrm{kg}$ car has a driving force of $1800\ \mathrm{N}$ and resistance of $600\ \mathrm{N}$. Its acceleration is $(1800-600)/1200=1.0\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$ forwards. At the same resistance, reducing the driving force to $600\ \mathrm{N}$ gives zero acceleration; reducing it to zero gives $-0.50\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$, so the forward-moving car decelerates.

With a constant driving force, increasing speed raises air resistance and reduces acceleration. If resistance grows to equal the driving force, the car reaches a constant maximum speed under those conditions: terminal motion need not be vertical.

## Method

Describing the motion of an object released from rest in a fluid:

1. List the vertical forces: weight downwards; drag upwards; upthrust upwards if it is in a liquid.
2. At release the speed is zero, so the drag is zero: the resultant force is the weight (minus upthrust) and $a = g$ in air.
3. As the speed rises, the drag rises, so the resultant force falls and **the acceleration falls while the speed still rises**.
4. When drag (plus upthrust) equals the weight, the resultant force is zero: constant terminal velocity.
5. For a numerical part, write $F_\mathrm{res} = mg - D$, then $a = F_\mathrm{res}/m$.

> [!example]- Worked example
> A skydiver of mass 75 kg falls vertically through air. At one instant the air resistance acting on her is 420 N. Calculate her acceleration at that instant.
>
> Weight: $W = mg = 75 \times 9.81 = 735.75\ \mathrm{N}$.
>
> Resultant force (downwards positive): $F_\mathrm{res} = W - D = 735.75 - 420 = 315.75\ \mathrm{N}$.
>
> Newton's second law:
> $$a = \frac{F_\mathrm{res}}{m} = \frac{315.75}{75} = 4.21\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\ \text{downwards}$$
>
> As she speeds up, $D$ increases, so $a$ decreases towards zero: she approaches terminal velocity.

## Traps

- **Trap:** air resistance makes a falling object slow down. **Why it's wrong:** while $W > D$ the resultant force is still downwards. **Instead:** the object keeps speeding up, with a *decreasing acceleration*.
- **Trap:** at terminal velocity the acceleration is $9.81\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}$. **Why it's wrong:** the forces balance, so the resultant force is zero. **Instead:** $a = 0$; the velocity, not the acceleration, is constant and non-zero.
- **Trap:** the drag force has one fixed value for a given fluid. **Why it's wrong:** drag comes from pushing fluid aside, so it depends on speed. **Instead:** a drag–speed graph starts at the origin and rises.
- **Trap:** forgetting upthrust in a liquid. **Why it's wrong:** three forces act, so $D = W$ is wrong. **Instead:** use $D + U = W$, and remember $U$ is constant as the body moves.
- **Trap:** terminal velocity depends on the height or depth it was dropped from. **Why it's wrong:** the balance $D + U = W$ contains no reference to distance fallen. **Instead:** only mass, size/shape and the fluid matter.

## Exam technique

*Describe* asks for the features of the motion (initial acceleration, decreasing acceleration, constant velocity); *Explain* demands the chain of reasoning — "speed increases → air resistance increases → resultant force decreases → acceleration decreases". Examiners repeatedly report that candidates state the acceleration decreases without saying the air resistance *increases* and the resultant force *decreases*; both links earn marks. On *Sketch* questions, make the velocity–time graph start with gradient $g$ and finish horizontal. When you *Calculate* with a resistive force, subtract it from the driving force or weight — never add it, and never use $g$ for a body moving along a horizontal surface.
