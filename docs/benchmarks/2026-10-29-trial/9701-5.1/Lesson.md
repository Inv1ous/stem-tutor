> [!quote] You
> I do not understand why the average bond energy differs from the exact bond energy of one bond. Is there a figure?

> [!abstract] In plain words
> An exact bond energy is for one specific bond in one specific gaseous molecule. An average bond energy is the mean for the same type of bond across different molecules. Because the surroundings of a bond change its energy, calculations using averages are estimates.

> [!abstract] The idea in more detail
> An exact bond dissociation enthalpy belongs to a specified bond-breaking process in a specified gaseous species, such as splitting gaseous hydrogen molecules into gaseous atoms. An average bond energy is a mean; a tabulated value commonly averages a bond type over different gaseous compounds.
>
> Molecular environment affects bond energy, so the same bond type need not have the same value everywhere. Successive bond dissociations in methane also differ: the mean C–H energy in methane is one quarter of its total gaseous atomisation enthalpy, not simply the first dissociation energy.
>
> Here exact means specific to a process, not free from experimental uncertainty.

> [!quote] You
> Can you work an example of finding the enthalpy change of burning methane from bond energies?

> [!tip] Tutor-written: Worked example: estimating the enthalpy change of combustion of methane from average bond energies, with bonds counted f
> Add up the energy needed to break all the bonds in the reactants, subtract the energy released when the bonds in the products form, and you get an estimate of about −818 kJ mol⁻¹ for burning methane (using the average bond energies below).
>
> 1. Write the balanced equation with every substance as a gas, because bond energies refer to gaseous molecules. Then draw out the bonds: CH₄ has 4 C–H, $O₂$ has O=O, CO₂ has 2 C=O, and H₂O has 2 O–H.
>
> $$
> \mathrm{CH_4(g) + 2O_2(g) \rightarrow CO_2(g) + 2H_2O(g)}
> $$
>
> 2. Count the bonds broken in the reactants: 4 C–H in one CH₄, and 2 O=O in two $O₂$. Use these average bond energies: C–H = 410, O=O = 496, C=O = 805 and O–H = 460, all in kJ mol⁻¹.
>
>    Check them against your own data table, as values vary slightly between sources.
>
> $$
> \text{Energy in} = (4\times 410) + (2\times 496) = 1640 + 992 = 2632\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}
> $$
>
> 3. Count the bonds formed in the products: 2 C=O in one CO₂, and 2 O–H in each of two H₂O, giving 4 O–H altogether.
>
> $$
> \text{Energy out} = (2\times 805) + (4\times 460) = 1610 + 1840 = 3450\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}
> $$
>
> 4. Combine them using $\Delta H = \text{bonds broken} - \text{bonds formed}$. Breaking bonds is endothermic and forming bonds is exothermic, so more energy released than absorbed gives a negative answer.
>
> $$
> \Delta H = 2632 - 3450 = -818\ \mathrm{kJ\ mol^{-1}}
> $$
>
> This is only an estimate. Tabulated values are average bond energies, taken over many different molecules, but the energy of a bond depends on its molecular environment.
>
> For example, the C–H bond in methane is not quite the same as in other compounds, and the C=O in CO₂ differs from C=O in other molecules. Also, the equation here gives gaseous water, whereas the standard enthalpy change of combustion refers to liquid water.
>
> The experimental value is therefore noticeably different (roughly −890 kJ mol⁻¹).

> [!quote] Your working for Q2
> Bond energies in the table are average values taken over many different molecules, so the real bond energy in ethanol is a bit different from the average. That means the calculation is only an estimate.

> [!abstract] Example answer
> The bond energies in a table are average values: each is the mean of a bond type measured in different molecules. The actual energy of a bond depends on its molecular environment, so it may differ from the average.
>
> The calculation is therefore an estimate and may not equal the experimental value.
