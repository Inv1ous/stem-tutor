> [!quote] You
> I cannot picture how two slits make bright and dark fringes. Is there a figure?

> [!abstract] In plain words
> To see steady fringes the waves must overlap and be coherent: the same frequency and a constant phase difference. Fringes are clearest when the amplitudes are similar, and for light a single wavelength helps.
>
> For sources in phase, a path difference of a whole number of wavelengths gives a maximum and a half-integer number gives a minimum.

> [!abstract] Helpful term
> **Path difference:** The difference between the distances travelled by the two waves to reach a point.
>
> Written: $n\lambda$ (maximum), $(n+\tfrac12)\lambda$ (minimum)
>
> Said: n lambda; n plus a half, lambda

> [!tip] Tutor-written: Worked example: finding fringe spacing x from x = lambda D / a for a double slit, with unit conversions to metres
> The fringe spacing $x$ is the distance between adjacent bright fringes (or adjacent dark ones) and is found from $x = \frac{\lambda D}{a}$. Convert every quantity to metres first, then substitute.
>
> **Worked example**
>
> Monochromatic light of wavelength $600\,\mathrm{nm}$ passes through two slits with separation $a = 0.40\,\mathrm{mm}$. The screen is $D = 2.0\,\mathrm{m}$ from the slits. Find the fringe spacing $x$.
>
> 1. Convert the wavelength to metres, using $1\,\mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}$.
>
> $$
> \lambda = 600\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m} = 6.0\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}
> $$
>
> 2. Convert the slit separation to metres, using $1\,\mathrm{mm} = 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}$. The distance $D$ is already in metres.
>
> $$
> a = 0.40\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m} = 4.0\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m}
> $$
>
> 3. Substitute into $x = \frac{\lambda D}{a}$ and evaluate.
>
> $$
> x = \frac{6.0\times10^{-7}\times 2.0}{4.0\times10^{-4}} = 3.0\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}
> $$
>
> 4. Give the result in a sensible unit: $x = 3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$. This means that neighbouring bright fringes are $3.0\,\mathrm{mm}$ apart on the screen, which is easy to see and measure.
>
