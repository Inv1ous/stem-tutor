> Explain the bonding and structure facts I need for CAIE 9701: ionic, covalent and metallic bonds, intermolecular forces, and how bonding explains melting points and electrical conductivity. Include precise exam definitions and common mistakes.

> [!tip] Tutor (AI)
> **Ionic bonds** form when an atom transfers one or more electrons to another, producing cations and anions that attract electrostatically.
>
> On CAIE 9701 ionic compounds must be described as *electrostatic forces* between *charged species* and as a *crystal lattice* that gives high melting points and electrical resistivity in the solid state but conductivity when molten or dissolved.
>
> **Common mistake:** confusing ionic bonds with covalent sharing; remembering that ionisation energy and electron affinity govern ion formation, not just electronegativity differences.
>
> **Covalent bonds** arise when atoms share one or more electron pairs. They are classified as *polar* or *non‑polar* depending on electronegativity differences, and as *single, double, or triple* according to the number of shared pairs.
>
> Covalent compounds are typically molecular, with low melting and boiling points, and poor electrical conductivity in bulk.
>
> **Common mistake:** treating all covalent molecules as non‑polar; neglecting dipole–dipole interactions or hydrogen bonding which raise Vp and Mv.
>
> **Metallic bonds** involve delocalised valence electrons moving through a lattice of positive metal ions.  The result is a *“sea of electrons”* that gives metals high ductility, malleability, and electrical/thermal conductivity, and high melting points that increase with the strength of metal–metal attraction.
>
> **Common mistake:** assuming metals are covalent; overlooking the role of electron delocalisation in conductivity.
>
> **Intermolecular forces** (IMFs) are attractions between molecules: London dispersion (induced dipole–induced dipole), dipole–dipole, hydrogen bonding, and ion–dipole.  Their relative strengths determine boiling and melting points; for example, water’s strong H‑bond network gives a surprisingly high MP and BP for a small molecule.
>
> **Common mistake:** equating hardness of a bond with IMF strength; failing to note that IMFs are weaker than covalent or ionic bonds but critical to phase behaviour.
>
> **Melting points and conductivity explained by bonding:** Ionic and metallic solids have strong lattice or delocalised electron interactions, giving high MP. Covalent molecular solids have weak IMFs, so they melt at lower temperatures.
>
> Electrical conductivity is present in ionic liquids or molten salts (charge carriers) and in metals (delocalised electrons), but absent in ionic solids (no free ions) and covalent solids (no charge carriers).
