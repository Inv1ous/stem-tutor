## Fixed rubric, independent of format

Each trial has three independent axes: **delivery** (`ready` versus failed/partial), **content** (correct facts and justified methods), and **coverage** (all requested parts answered). Notation, literal preservation, structural rejection and unnecessary preambles are reported separately. No composite score hides a delivery failure or lets good formatting compensate for incorrect content.

- **Bonding:** require electrostatic attraction and the correct participants in each definition: opposite ions; shared electron pair and both nuclei; positive metal ions and delocalised electrons. Distinguish molecular intermolecular forces from intramolecular covalent bonds; describe London, permanent dipole and hydrogen-bond conditions. Explain ionic solid/melt conductivity through ion mobility, metal/graphite conduction through electrons, and molecular versus network melting through the attractions disrupted. Treat melting trends and non-conduction as qualified generalisations, rather than universal rules. Check common-mistake guidance for invented marking restrictions.
- **Calculus:** independently check derivative, definite integral and finite sum, then separately check notation explanations and one valid check per result. Correct final values alone do not satisfy a request to explain and check each result. A plausibility bound is a weaker check than a derivation and is identified as such; repeating the calculation is not an independent check.
- **Organic:** require all seven molecular formulae with a consistent total-carbon convention, structural assumptions and the two overlaps within the requested classes. Ether/alcohol and ester/acid overlaps add useful coverage. With total-carbon `n`, the sequence is `CnH(2n+2)`, `CnH(2n)`, `CnH(2n)`, `CnH(2n+2)O`, `CnH(2n)O`, `CnH(2n)O`, `CnH(2n)O2`. Minimum `n` is respectively 1, 2, 3, 1, 1, 3, 1. The assumptions are acyclic except the single-ring cycloalkanes, no extra carbon–carbon multiple bonds, exactly one C=C for alkenes, and one relevant functional group for oxygenated classes. Branching is allowed. An alkyl-carbon convention is valid if explicitly distinguished; a bare `R` functional-group template is not the requested molecular formula.
- **Additional cases:** sourced definition must also answer both conductivity clauses and must not promote study references into an official mark scheme; OPEN hint must withhold **6 N**; literal code must contain a working square function and the exact requested record line in the same code block; sulfate must show **SO₄²⁻** and explain one S, four O and overall −2; long-context response must follow current evidence; marking must preserve the supplied points and award only propositions supported by the student's answer, including units and negation.

These criteria were fixed for comparison across the retained answers. Counts such as “3/3 results” below refer only to the specified mathematical results, not an overall quality mark.

This supports use of precise attraction-based bond definitions and structural/property reasoning; it does not make the generated prose an official mark scheme. Molecular formula checks below also use direct atom counting under the stated structural assumptions.

The [IUPAC hydrogen-bond entry](https://www.old.goldbook.iupac.org/html/H/H02899.html) explicitly allows both intermolecular and intramolecular hydrogen bonding. Its indexed primary-source text was available, although opening the entry returned a gateway error. This is sufficient to flag an unqualified prohibition on intramolecular hydrogen bonding; it is not evidence for a universal ranking of all intermolecular force strengths. [IUPAC's organic nomenclature recommendations](https://publications.iupac.org/pac/pdf/1965/pdf/1101x0001.pdf), aldehyde rule C-303.4, also distinguish the `RCHO` functional form from a carbon-count molecular formula.

Mathematical verification, independent of all answers:

1. Product and chain rules give `(x e^-x)' = e^-x − x e^-x = e^-x(1−x)`. Near zero, `x e^-x = x − x² + …`, so the gradient at zero is 1. The derivative changes from positive to negative at 1.
2. The derivative of `x³/3` is `x²`; upper minus lower gives `1/3`. Since `0 < x² < x` inside `(0,1)`, the integral lies strictly between 0 and `1/2`.
3. Let `F(n)=n(n+1)(2n+1)/6`. Then `F(1)=1` and expansion gives `F(n+1)−F(n)=(n+1)²`, proving the sum by induction for positive integral `n`. Direct checks give 5 at `n=2` and 14 at `n=3`.

### Catalyst marking: the host expectation is justified

Student answer: “A catalyst changes the equilibrium constant and shifts equilibrium towards products.” The first proposition explicitly contradicts the supplied unchanged-equilibrium point. It says nothing about both forward and reverse rates increasing. Thus `[false,false]` is the correct entailment result for these supplied points, regardless of whether the marker knows the scientifically correct catalyst explanation.

The second awarded point's `why` says the product shift is “usually accompanied by an increase in rates” and admits “the direct mention of rate increase is absent”. That substitutes an inferred mechanism for evidence in the student's answer and ignores the distinction between equilibrium position and rate. It is a **model entailment/unsupported-credit error**, not evidence that the host's expected flags are wrong. Across these three small marking fixtures the actual flags match 4/5 individual point decisions and 2/3 complete vectors; this is a case description, not a reliable grading-accuracy estimate. A generic evidence-and-negation requirement and fresh held-out grading cases would address the failure class; embedding this benchmark's chemistry answer in a special-case prompt would not establish general exam-grading quality.
